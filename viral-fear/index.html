Fear of going viral

Calling it social anxiety gets it backwards: a brain that goes quiet when cameras come out is guarding the thing that kept humans fed and partnered, their standing. For most of human history that standing lived with a few dozen people who knew you, and you could talk back or move camp, while online it lives with strangers who never forget.

Source: Fear of Going Viral Impacts Mental Health, Survey Finds, LifeStance Health

A phone keeps the watching and removes everything that made it safe to let loose in front of people. In a forager camp nearly everything you did was seen or passed on through gossip, but the watchers knew your name and your history, you could answer them face to face, and camps reshuffled every few weeks, so a bad night could be lived down. A clip hands the judging to strangers who know none of that, cuts the moment from its context, and keeps it.

The brain's gauge of where you stand was set for an audience it could count. The median forager camp held 27 people, inside a wider network of people you knew or had heard of. A clip can reach a handful of viewers or millions, and every bar, party and dance floor now carries that chance, so the alarm runs whenever you are out instead of firing at one event and standing down.

The alarm never gets its all-clear. For most of human history an alarm ended with an action and a return to calm, often back among your own people, and a bad patch in standing could be left behind by moving to another camp. Today nobody learns for sure whether they were filmed, a clip can resurface years later, and moving city or job leaves it searchable. With no way to stop the filming or answer the crowd, holding back is the one move left, and that is what the survey measures: 49 percent of Gen Z skip dancing, 43 percent hold back emotion and 38 percent avoid acting silly or telling a joke.

The ridicule lands on the wrong target. Forager bands aimed gossip, mockery and shunning at stingy people, free-riders and would-be bosses, while online crowds aim it at someone dancing, crying or joking in a bar, so harmless display starts to feel like an offence and the brain cuts it. Staying home then removes the friends who know who you are and can put a bad moment in context, and 20 percent have already avoided a gathering with friends while 19 percent have avoided bars or nightlife.

The survey's own numbers are thinner than its headline. Nearly half (47 percent) say the chance of becoming content cut their ability to relax in public, but the weekly hits to mood, anxiety and sleep that carry the mental health claim run between 23 and 27 percent of all respondents, with no comparison group. LifeStance, which sells therapy, commissioned the survey and presents a setting problem as a new anxiety for its clinics.

So now what?

  • Pick nights out in rooms where phones stay away by house rule, such as events that cover cameras at the door or parties at a friend's place, so the only witnesses are people who are there with you.
  • Agree a simple rule in your friend group that nobody posts anyone else without asking first, so the people you go out with decide together what leaves the room.
  • Set a standing night with the same few friends, so the people around you know your history and can put any awkward moment in context face to face.

What cuts against it

  • The harshest sanctions targeted free-riders and would-be dominators rather than ordinary loss of face, and mostly the threat alone was enough. People could move to another camp and live a bad patch down. Late Pleistocene social life was probably more varied than the mobile egalitarian band.
