What really is:

Borderline personality disorder

What it really is

A strong fear of being left, and only one person to lose.

Borderline personality disorder is what psychiatry calls people who panic when someone pulls away, rage when they are let down and can't stand being alone. Every human has that fear. For nearly all of human history a child left alone died, and an adult who lost their partner lost a mate and an ally. Some people are born with a stronger fear than others, and the difference is inherited. In a camp you had dozens of people around you all day, so when one of them pulled away the others were still there. Now most adults lean on one partner, and every late reply is a threat to the only bond they have. No blood test or scan can show a disorder. Nothing is wrong with the personality. It is a sensitive one, living with one person where there should be a camp.

The part of you that panics when your people leave is 165 million years old. Every mammal has it. A pup that loses its mother cries for her. People have always differed in how strongly it goes off, and the difference is inherited. Humans have existed for 300,000 years, and for nearly all of that time you lived among a few dozen people who knew you, with about five close ones to lean on. Then people started farming and settling down, and villages grew into towns and cities. That began 12,000 years ago. Now most adults live among strangers and lean on one partner. If that person cools off, there is nobody else, so the fear goes off at full strength, and a doctor calls it a personality disorder.

How it happens

  1. 1

    Every human fears being left.

    The science says

    Being left alone is the most dangerous thing that can happen to a young mammal, and an adult who lost their partner lost a mate and an ally. So every human fears being left.

    Panksepp describes the same separation-distress system across mammals, and in some birds. Bowlby described what small children do when their mother disappears: loud protest first, then despair, then a child that barely reacts when she comes back.

    The science says

    People differ in how strongly that fear goes off, and the difference is inherited. The home you grew up in doesn't explain it.

    In Swedish records on 1.85 million people, genes explained 46 percent of who got the diagnosis, and growing up in the same home explained none of it. Every behavioural trait that has been measured is partly inherited. Two twin studies compared twins where one was abused and the other wasn't, and found little to no effect of the abuse on who ended up with the pattern.

So now what

  1. Ask a doctor to check for a physical cause, such as a thyroid fault, and say how you sleep. Long-term bad sleep produces similar symptoms.
  2. One partner can't be a whole camp. Add people: a team, a choir, a club, neighbours. Anything that meets every week with the same faces.
  3. Pick one or two people who have shown that they stay, and see them on fixed days every week. A fixed day takes away the waiting and the guessing.
  4. If you want therapy, the kinds tested for this give you the same person every week for a year. In a trial of 180 patients two different kinds did equally well, so choose the person and stay.
  5. Tell the people close to you what the first sign looks like in you and what helps when it starts.

If you take drugs for this: no drug is approved for it and trials found none that works on it, so where no test shows a physical disease the goal is to come off them. Do it as a slow taper supervised by your prescriber, over months, while you work on the rest. Never stop suddenly. Stopping some of these drugs at once can bring withdrawal symptoms that are sometimes severe. If you are thinking about ending your life or hurting yourself, tell someone today and call your local emergency number.

What cuts against it

  • Most of the research points to childhood. Across 97 studies, seven in ten patients reported childhood adversity, and they were 14 times as likely to report it as people with no diagnosis. The twin studies that found no effect of abuse measured borderline traits in young adults from the general population, so they say less about diagnosed patients and about severe abuse.

What would prove this wrong

If people who live among dozens of others who know them, with several close ones, show this pattern as often as people who lean on one partner, this page is wrong.

Nobody has measured it. No study has counted this pattern among hunter-gatherers.

Strongest objection"I was abused as a child. Are you saying that doesn't matter?" It matters. Abuse does harm, and nothing here excuses it. The twin studies say one narrow thing: when one twin was abused and the other wasn't, the abuse made little to no difference to who ended up with this pattern. You were born with a strong fear, and then people gave it reasons.
