What it really is

A strong fear of being left, and only one person to lose.

Borderline personality disorder is what psychiatry calls people who panic when someone pulls away, rage when they are let down and can't stand being alone. Every human has that fear. For nearly all of human history a child left alone died, and an adult who lost their partner lost a mate and an ally.Some people are born with a stronger fear than others, and about half of the difference is inherited. In a camp you had dozens of people around you all day, so when one of them pulled away the others were still there. Now most adults lean on one partner, and every late reply is a threat to the only bond they have.No blood test or scan can show a disorder. Nothing is wrong with the personality. It is a sensitive one, living with one person where there should be a camp.

The part of you that panics when your people leave is 165 million years old.Learn moreClose
Every mammal has it. A pup that loses its mother cries for her. People have always differed in how strongly it goes off, and about half of the difference is inherited. Humans have existed for 300,000 years, and for nearly all of that time you lived among a few dozen people who knew you, with about five close ones to lean on. Then people started farming and settling down, and villages grew into towns and cities. That began 12,000 years ago. Now most adults live among strangers and lean on one partner. If that person cools off, there is nobody else, so the fear goes off at full strength, and a doctor calls it a personality disorder.

What it is not

A disordered personalityThe psychiatric manual DSM-5: a lasting pattern of unstable relationships, self-image and emotions.A personality doesn't wear off. This does. Researchers followed 290 patients for 16 years, and 99 percent of them stopped meeting the criteria for two years or more. After two years without it, 36 percent met the criteria again. The name was coined in 1938 for patients who didn't get better in psychoanalysis. The usual treatment failed, and the patients got a name for it. The name put them on the border between two older labels, neurosis and psychosis, and no test stood behind either. Two members of the World Health Organization group that revised the personality disorder classification say science does not support the diagnosis and it should be dropped.

How it happens

  1. 1

    Every human fears being left.

    The science says

    Being left alone is the most dangerous thing that can happen to a young mammal, and an adult who lost their partner lost a mate and an ally. So every human fears being left.

    Panksepp describes the same separation-distress system across mammals, and in some birds. Bowlby described what small children do when their mother disappears: loud protest first, then despair, then a child that barely reacts when she comes back.

The cost-benefit analysis

Your brain does a cost-benefit analysis on every sign that someone is pulling away. Reacting to a sign that meant nothing costs a scene. Missing a real one costs you the person. So the brain reacts early, and in people born with a stronger fear it reacts earlier and harder. In a camp, dozens of others were still there when one person pulled away, so a missed sign cost less. Now most adults lean on one partner. A missed sign costs your mate and your only ally at once, so the brain goes off at every late reply.

So now what

  1. Ask a doctor to check for a physical cause, such as a thyroid fault, and say how you sleep. Two members of the WHO group that revised the personality disorder classification say long-term bad sleep creates the same symptoms.
  2. One partner can't be a whole camp. Add people: a team, a choir, a club, neighbours. Anything that meets every week with the same faces.
  3. Pick one or two people who have shown that they stay, and see them on fixed days every week. A fixed day takes away the waiting and the guessing.
  4. If you want therapy, the kind matters little. No kind has beaten the others, and in a trial of 180 patients a year of a therapy built for this diagnosis did no better than a year of general psychiatric care. Pick a therapist you can stay with.
  5. Tell the people close to you what the first sign looks like in you and what helps when it starts.

If you take drugs for this: no drug is approved for it and in trials none beat a sugar pill on it. Where no test shows a physical disease, the aim is to replace the drug with the real thing: people who need you, give back and stay. The drugs go as those people come in. Bring the dose down with a doctor while you build that. It doesn't have to be the doctor who prescribed them. Never stop suddenly. Stopping some of these drugs at once can bring withdrawal symptoms that are sometimes severe. If you are thinking about ending your life or hurting yourself, tell someone today and call your local emergency number.
