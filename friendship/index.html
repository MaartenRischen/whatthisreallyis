What really is:

Friendship

What it really is

Two people who help each other survive, get laid, or both.

Friendship is an alliance between two people who aren't family. Each one helps the other and expects it back, and each brain keeps count. You feel the count as liking, gratitude, guilt and resentment. For nearly all of human history you needed friends for food, for care when you were sick or hurt, and for finding a mate. Now you can buy the food and the care from strangers and use an app for the mate, so most friendships have nothing at stake.

The part of you that makes friends is 25 million years old. Apes and many monkeys have it too. Humans have existed for 300,000 years, and for nearly all of that time most of the people you lived with weren't family. You shared food with them. When you were too sick or hurt to find your own food, they fed you and looked after you. The more allies you had, the better your chances of a mate. Then people started farming and settling down, and villages grew into towns and cities. That began 12,000 years ago. Now you buy your food, your lift home and your care when you're ill, and an app finds your dates. Your friends have nothing they need to do for you, so the friendship goes quiet, and people call that growing apart.

Then and now

For 300,000 years, you needed friends for food, for care when you were sick or hurt, and for finding a mate.

Now you can buy all of that from strangers. You don't need your friends and they don't need you, so you drift apart.

How it happens

  1. 1

    Helping someone who isn't family only pays off if they help you back.

    The science says

    Helping someone who isn't family costs you something. It only pays off if they help you back, so humans are built to help the people who return it.

    Trivers showed in 1971 that helping non-relatives can evolve when the same few individuals keep meeting over a long life and stop helping the ones who don't return it. He named friendship, gratitude, guilt and suspicion as the feelings that run it.

    argued Trivers RL (1971). The Evolution of Reciprocal Altruism. Quarterly Review of Biology 46.Cor M11, Cooperation / Alliance: 123 graded findings
  2. so2

    For 300,000 years you lived mostly with people who weren't family, and you picked the ones who helped back.

    The science says

    For 300,000 years most of the people you lived with weren't family. You chose who to live with, and you chose the ones who helped back.

    Close relatives made up less than 10 percent of a forager camp. Food sharing is found in every forager society studied. Hadza camps moved every four to eight weeks, and people kept choosing who to live with.

    You needed them for food on the days you found none, for care when you were too sick or hurt to look after yourself, and for a better chance of a mate.

    Among the Ache, people who had shared more got food from more people when they were sick or injured. Tooby and Cosmides call this the banker's paradox: you need help most at the moment you look least able to repay it, so it pays to have people who can't replace you. In 148 studies, people with strong relationships were 50 percent more likely to be alive at follow-up.

Real example cases

I was always the one to ask if friends were free.

A mother writing for AARP's The Girlfriend about being the only one who reaches out

She writes that she kept no score as a child and started to as an adult. Her brain was counting the whole time. She only felt it once the count stayed one-sided.

So now what

  1. Ask a friend for help with something real: a move, a lift, a repair. Do the same for them.
  2. Pick a few people and see them every week, in person.
  3. When a friend is in trouble, show up. They will remember it.

Don't start counting favours out loud. Counting out loud is what strangers do.

Send this to the friend you haven't heard from since you stopped texting first.

Why it wins. Trading help explains 6 of the 9 details: liking people who like you back, the guilt, the resentment, why paying a friend feels wrong, why help in a crisis is remembered, and why friendships fade when nothing is at stake. Getting attached explains the hours it takes and the limit of about five.

The 9 details
  1. You like people who like you back.
  2. You feel guilty when you owe a friend.
  3. You resent the friend who never calls first.
  4. Paying a friend for a favour feels wrong.
  5. Help in a crisis is the help people remember.
  6. Friendships fade when the job or the class ends.
  7. Most people have about five close friends.
  8. A close friend takes more than 200 hours.
  9. People with close ties live longer.

What cuts against it

  • Much forager sharing happened because others asked for it and refusing was hard. Peterson argues that this is pressure, and that it works differently from give and take.

    argued Peterson N (1993). Demand sharing: Reciprocity and the pressure for generosity among foragers. American Anthropologist 95.

  • DeScioli and Kurzban argue that friendship is closer to picking allies for future conflicts than to trading favours. In their data, the favours a friend did predicted the friend's rank less well than where you stood with that friend.

    DeScioli P, Kurzban R (2009). The Alliance Hypothesis for Human Friendship. PLoS ONE 4(6). *

  • The Ache result comes from one settled community, and the Hadza figures from one population. A later Hadza study found no nutritional benefit to being a well-reputed hunter.

    tested Gurven M, Allen-Arave W, Hill K, Hurtado M (2000). "It's a Wonderful Life": signaling generosity among the Ache of Paraguay. Evolution and Human Behavior 21.tested Apicella CL, Marlowe FW, Fowler JH, Christakis NA (2012). Social networks and cooperation in hunter-gatherers. Nature 481.

What would prove this wrong

If people stay just as close to friends who stop giving anything back, this page is wrong.

In three studies, how people ranked a friend followed how they thought that friend ranked them.

Strongest objection"So friendship is just using each other?" No. You do care about your friends, and they care about you. The caring is what makes each of you help when the other can't pay it back.
