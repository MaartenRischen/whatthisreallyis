What it really is

Good feelings pull you towards whatever kept your ancestors alive and got them children. Bad feelings push you away from whatever got them killed or cost them children.

Food when you're hungry, sex, a friend who shares, winning, a baby smiling at you: all of it feels good, so you go after it again. A predator, rotten meat, being cheated, being left out, losing a child: all of it feels bad, so you get away from it, fight it off or fix it.Nobody had to understand why. The ones who felt it that way had more children, and you descend from them. Emotions are not there to make you happy.Now sugar, porn and gambling apps are built to feel good and get you nothing. Debt, an inbox and strangers on a screen feel bad, and you can't fight them or run from them. Then you are told your emotions are the problem and sold a way to manage them.

The part of you that makes you freeze, run or fight back is 460 million years old, and every animal with a backbone has it.Learn moreClose
The part that makes you miss your people is 165 million years old, and every mammal has it. Humans have existed for 300,000 years, and for nearly all of that time you lived in a camp of a few dozen people who knew you. What set off a feeling was near you, and you could go after it or deal with it. Then people started farming and settling down, and villages grew into towns and cities. That began 12,000 years ago. Now you spend the day among strangers. What sets you off is a deadline, a debt or a post by someone you will never meet. You can't deal with any of them, and people call that poor emotional regulation.

An emotion also picks what you look at, what you remember and what you want, so your thinking happens inside it.

Then and now

For 300,000 years, what felt good kept you alive and got you children, and what felt bad was a danger you could get away from, fight off or fix.

Now a bet on a phone feels good and gets you nothing. A debt feels bad, and you can't fight it or run from it.

    Take fear. When you think someone is following you, you hear small sounds you would normally miss. Safety pushes every other goal aside.   4. so4

    Several are also messages to the people around you, and for 300,000 years those people saw them and did something.

    The science says

    In experiments people paid their own money to punish someone who cheated the group, with nothing to gain. Anger at the cheat drove it. Where punishing was allowed, cooperation held. Where it was ruled out, cooperation fell apart. Shame tracks what other people think. In three countries the shame people expected to feel matched how much others would think less of them. Looking down and blushing tell the others you accept the lower place, which makes them less likely to keep attacking. Guilt makes you repair what you broke, and gratitude makes you pay back. A young mammal separated from its mother cries and searches for her. A camp held about 30 people who all knew each other, and nearly everything a person did was seen or heard about by people who knew them. Each person had about five close people to lean on. Among the !Kung, who lived by hunting and gathering, a crying baby got a fast response, usually from the mother and often from someone else. !Kung babies cried as often as Dutch babies and for half as long. It works on adults too. Holding a trusted person's hand lowers the brain's reaction to a threat.

    The science says

In 2.7 million Facebook and Twitter posts, each word about the other political side went with 67 percent higher odds of a share, and with angry reactions. An alarm that never switches off damages the heart and blood vessels and the immune system.

The cost-benefit analysis

Your brain does a cost-benefit analysis on everything that happens to you, and a feeling is the result. Good means this paid for your ancestors, so go after it again. Bad means this cost them, so get away from it, fight it off or fix it. The sums were settled over millions of years, on what things cost and paid back then. Sugar, porn and gambling apps are built to come out as good and get you nothing. Debt, an inbox and strangers on a screen come out as bad, and you can't fight them or run from them.

So now what

  1. When a strong feeling comes, ask what set it off before you try to make it stop. What happened, who did it, and what did you lose or nearly lose?
  2. Where you can change what set it off, change that: the person, the job, the debt.
  3. Feel it next to someone who knows you, in the same room. A phone call does more than a text.
  4. Cut what sets you off and leaves you nothing to do about it. Posts about what the other side did get shared more and draw angry reactions, and there is nothing you can do with that anger.
  5. When something feels good, ask what it gets you. Sugar, porn and a bet on your phone get you nothing. Go after what the feeling is for: food that feeds you, a partner, people who need you.
  6. See a doctor if a strong feeling started suddenly with no change in your life, or soon after a new pill. Infections, hormone problems, drugs and head injuries can set off fear or low mood.

If you take pills for anxiety or low mood: where no test shows a physical disease, the aim is to replace the pill with the real thing, such as people who need you and give back. The pill goes as that comes in. Bring the dose down with a doctor while you build it. It doesn't have to be the doctor who put you on the pills. Never stop suddenly. If you are thinking about ending your life, tell someone today and call your local emergency number.

What cuts against it

  • Researchers have looked for the mark of each emotion in the face, the body and the brain, and found no single pattern for any of them. Lisa Feldman Barrett concludes that emotions are made from more basic parts: the body, a brain that wires itself to its surroundings, and culture. What would prove this wrong

If how strongly people feel an emotion had nothing to do with what is at stake for them, this page is wrong.

It has been tested for shame. In three countries and in 15 small communities, the shame people expected to feel matched how much others would think less of them.
