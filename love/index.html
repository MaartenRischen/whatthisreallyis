What it really is

Your body attaching you to the people you need to survive, have children, or both.

Love is your body attaching you to a few people and keeping you close to them. A baby attaches to whoever looks after it. An adult attaches to a partner the same way, and two more things come with it: wanting sex, and fixing on one person.For nearly all of human history that got you looked after as a child, got you a mate, and kept the two of you together while your children were small. You did it inside a camp of a few dozen people. Now finding a partner is your own job, the most common place to meet one is online among strangers, and married people see their family and friends less than single people do. One person is expected to be everything.

The part of you that gets attached is 165 million years old.Learn moreClose
Every mammal has it, and it is what keeps a baby close to its mother. Humans have existed for 300,000 years, and for nearly all of that time a couple lived in a camp of a few dozen people who knew them both. People in the camp shared food and helped raise the children. In most hunter-gatherer societies studied, your family arranged your marriage. Then people started farming and settling down, and villages grew into towns and cities. That began 12,000 years ago. Now finding a partner is your own job, and the most common place to meet one is online, among strangers. Married people see their family and friends less than single people do. One person has to be your lover, your best friend, your co-parent and your family. When one person can't be all of that, people call it falling out of love.

Then and now

For 300,000 years, a couple lived in a camp of a few dozen people who shared food and helped raise the children.

Now married people see family and friends less than single people do, and each asks the other for the company and the care a camp used to give. One person can't be a whole camp, and people call that falling out of love.

How it happens

  1. 1

    Babies attach to whoever looks after them.

    The science says

    A human baby can't survive alone, so babies attach to whoever looks after them, and adults attach back.

    A baby's bond with its mother is a drive of its own, separate from being fed. Baby mammals cry out when they are separated from their mother, and the brain network behind it is much the same across mammals. Raising one human child takes 13 million calories, more than a mother can find alone.

    Adults attach to a partner the same way, and add sex and fixing on one person.

    The science says

    Adults attach to a partner the same way. Two more drives come with it: wanting sex, and fixing on one person.

    In a study of 620 adults, people fell into the same three attachment patterns as babies, in close to the same proportions: 56 percent secure, 25 percent avoidant, 19 percent anxious. There are three systems for mating: sex drive, romantic love and attachment. They can run separately, so a person can be attached to one partner, in love with a second and want sex with a third. Brain scans of 17 people newly in love showed activity in two areas tied to reward and wanting. For most people the obsessive part fades within a few years.

    The science says

    Now finding a partner is your own job, and the most common place to meet one is online, among strangers. Married people see their family and friends less than single people do. One person is supposed to be lover, friend and the one who hears your troubles. When they can't, people call it falling out of love.

    Of straight American couples who met in 2017, 39 percent met online. In 1995 it was 2 percent. Online passed meeting through friends in 2013. Of the couples who met online, 89 percent had no connection to each other before. Meeting through family fell from 15 percent to 7. On one American dating site the most-contacted woman got 1,504 messages in a month, and most people wrote to someone 25 percent more desirable than themselves. Never-married Americans are far more likely than married ones to see their parents, brothers, sisters, neighbours and friends often, and the time married Americans spent with friends or relatives without their spouse fell between 1975 and 2003. Americans now ask a marriage for friendship, closeness, sex and personal growth more than earlier generations did. Care that many hands once shared now falls on one or two adults.

So now what

  1. Ask less of one person. See two or three other close people every week, in person, and take some of your troubles to them.
  2. Live your life as a couple in front of people who know you both. Eat with family, friends and neighbours, and let them help with the children.
  3. If you no longer think about your partner all day, look at what is still there before you decide anything. Are you calmer with them in the room? Would it hurt if they left?
  4. If you are looking for someone, ask the people who know you to introduce you.

If your partner hits you or threatens you, being attached is no reason to stay. Tell someone you trust and call a local helpline.

What would prove this wrong

If people who have a partner and nobody else do as well as people who have a partner and several other close ties, this page is wrong.

In a review of 148 studies, having many kinds of close ties went with much better survival. Living with someone, by itself, went with little.

Strongest objection"So you're saying love isn't real?" No. The attachment is real, and so is the pain when it breaks. The made-up parts are the one right person and the idea that one person can be everything.
