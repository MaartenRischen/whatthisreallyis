How it happens

  1. 1

    Your body learns a danger in one go.

    The science says

    Your body learns a danger in one go, and it is built to go off too often. A false alarm costs you a fright. A missed danger costs you your life.

    In rats, the fast route into the brain's danger centre learns a threat in one to three goes. Humans have the same centre, which is at least 340 million years old and shared by every four-limbed animal, and brain scans and injury studies show the same learning in people. Nesse worked out the sum: when running costs a thousand times less than being caught, the best setting runs from 999 false alarms for every real danger.

    It never unlearns it. It can learn that you are safe now.

    The science says

    It never unlearns the danger. What it learns on top is that you are safe now, by meeting the reminders again and again while nothing bad happens. That lesson holds better next to someone you trust.

    In rats and mice, a fear that has been trained away comes back in a new place, after time, or after a new scare, and the same return was measured in people. In an experiment with 30 students, a fear that was trained away next to a photo of the person who supports them most did not come back the next day. Trained away next to a stranger's photo, it did. Holding a hand lowers the brain's reaction to a threat.

The cost-benefit analysis

Your brain does a cost-benefit analysis on every reminder of what nearly killed you. A false alarm costs you a fright. A missed danger costs you your life. So it sounds the alarm at anything that looks like the first time. It does a second sum on going back. Going near a reminder costs fear now, and it is how your body learns that you are safe. Staying away costs nothing today. Next to someone you trust a threat looks smaller, so going back is cheaper, and for nearly all of human history those people were there. Alone, the brain picks staying away, and the lesson that it is over never comes.

So now what

  1. If the danger hasn't ended, the fear is right. Get away from the danger first.
  2. Tell the people close to you what happened, and pick the ones who will believe you. Find people who were there or who went through the same. They need no explaining.
  3. Go back to the places and things you avoid, a little at a time, next to someone you trust. Your body learns it is safe by being there while nothing bad happens.
  4. If you are stuck, get therapy that goes back over what happened. It does the same thing with a trained person next to you, and in trials it works.
  5. Ask a doctor to check for a physical cause, such as a head injury from the same event.

If you take drugs for this: where no physical defect is the cause, the aim is to replace the drug with the real thing, which is people who know what happened, need you and give back. The drug comes down as those people come in, because the real thing is hard to build while you are on it. Never stop suddenly. Stopping some of these drugs, and alcohol, at once is dangerous, so do that with a doctor. If you are thinking about ending your life or hurting yourself, tell someone today and call your local emergency number.

Why it wins. A threat system that learned a real danger explains 8 of the 12 details: the fear that lasts, the single lesson, the reminders, the jumpiness, the avoiding, the recovery of most people, the therapy that works and the weight of violence by other people. Who stays stuck depends more on two things the event did not bring: how easily the threat system was born to go off, and the people you came back to. Your people explain the ones nobody knows about, the ones who get no support and why going back over it works better next to someone. Tracking where you stand explains the shame, the hiding and the support that doesn't come. Nothing here explains how the word spread to everything that hurts. The people who sell it explain that.

The 12 details
  1. The fear stays on for years after the danger is over.
  2. One event is enough.
  3. A reminder sets it off: a shirt, a word, a place.
  4. You are jumpy, on edge and angry, and you sleep badly.
  5. You avoid everything that reminds you of it.
  6. Hardly anyone around you knows what happened.
  7. You blame yourself and feel ashamed.
  8. Most people who live through such an event recover.
  9. People with little support afterwards do worse.
  10. Going back over it with a therapist lowers the fear.
  11. The worst cases come from violence by other people: rape and war.
  12. The word now covers anything that hurt, most of all in childhood.

What would prove this wrong

If people who come back from danger to dozens of people who know them and know what happened stay stuck as often as people who come back to strangers, this page is wrong.

A study can settle this.

Strongest objection"I was raped. Are you telling me I'm fine?" No. What happened was real, and the fear is correct. Of all the events in the surveys, rape leaves the most people stuck: one in five. Your body is doing what it is built to do after that. Blame and disbelief from the people around you go with more symptoms later, and support goes with fewer.
