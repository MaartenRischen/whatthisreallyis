The three checks, for every finding in Cor:

  1. Every finding is checked against the study's full text or abstract. Summaries, search snippets and Wikipedia don't count.
  2. The finding says no more than the study does.
  3. Every grade names the strongest study behind it and that study's main weakness.

A grade never goes up because it would help a page. A finding that failed to replicate is marked down, however widely it is cited. Cor keeps records of the evidence against itself, publishes the strongest attacks on its own work and names the results that would kill it.

What it really is

Loneliness is nobody needing you.

Loneliness is what you feel when nobody depends on you. For nearly all of human history you lived among a few dozen people who knew you, and you counted on each other for food, for safety and for help when you were sick. Help went to the people the others counted on. Among the Ache of Paraguay, adults who gave away more of their food got food from more people when they were sick or hurt. Among Agta and BaYaka foragers, mothers who were close to everyone in the camp had more living children for their age. So the feeling comes fast and it hurts.Now you buy your food and your care from strangers. You can see friends every week and come home to a partner, and still nobody's day depends on you. Americans who live alone see friends and neighbours more than married people do. In Sweden and Finland more than four homes in ten hold one person, and northern Europe reports the least serious loneliness in Europe.A chatbot answers at any hour and needs nothing back, and in a trial with 981 adults the people who used one most ended up lonelier. A programme that put lonely people into groups that met and did things together cut loneliness three times as much as the usual care.Loneliness is no illness and no epidemic. Nothing in you is broken.

Humans have existed for 300,000 years, and for nearly all of that time you lived among a few dozen people who shared food with you, fed you when you were sick and counted on you to do the same. Then people started farming and settling down, and villages grew into towns and cities. That began 12,000 years ago. Now you pay strangers for your food and your care, a quarter of American adults eat every meal alone, and in Britain nearly one home in three holds one person. The feeling gets no answer, and the US government calls it an epidemic.

How it happens

  1. 1

    Help went to the people the others counted on.

    The science says

    You depended on the people around you for food when you were sick or hurt, for safety and for a mate, and they depended on you. Help went to the people the others counted on. So your body treats having nobody who needs you as an emergency.

    Among the Ache of Paraguay, adults who gave away a larger share of their food got food from more people when they were sick or hurt. Among the Shiwiar, most adults had come through an illness or injury that would probably have killed them if others hadn't fed them. Isolated people still die sooner. In 90 studies that followed 2.2 million adults, the socially isolated were 1.32 times as likely to die in the years studied. Cacioppo, who spent his career on loneliness, read the feeling as a warning like hunger or pain that pushes you back to your people.

    The feeling works like hunger.

    The science says

    The feeling works like hunger. It rises when you have been away from your people and falls when you are back with them.

    Forty adults spent ten hours alone, with no contact and no social media. 36 of them felt lonelier, and a reward centre in the midbrain answered pictures of people the way it answered pictures of food after ten hours of fasting. People differ in how much company they need, and about half of that difference is inherited.

    Companies sell stand-ins that quiet it for an evening.

The cost-benefit analysis

Your brain does a cost-benefit analysis on having nobody who needs you. For nearly all of human history a person the others didn't count on got less help when sick or hurt, so the brain prices it high and makes it hurt until people depend on you again. Being needed came with living among your people. Now it takes work: turning up for the same people every week and taking on things they can't do without you. A chatbot or a feed costs nothing and quiets the feeling for an evening. The brain takes it, you stay in, and nobody comes to depend on you.

So now what

  1. Be needed. Help a neighbour move, cook for someone, join a team that can't play without you. People stay close to the ones they need.
  2. See the same few people every week, in person, on fixed days. The same faces count. New ones every time don't.
  3. Eat with people. Live with people if you can, or next door to them.
  4. A club or a class counts by what it gives you: the same people, every week, doing something together. A programme that got lonely people into groups cut their loneliness far more than the usual care did.
  5. Don't wait until you feel like it. The longer the hunger lasts, the less you want to see anyone.

A chatbot, a feed or a screen full of strangers is no start. It takes the evening you needed for people. If you are thinking about ending your life, tell someone today and call your local emergency number.

Why it wins. Helping each other explains 7 of the 12 details: the room full of people that doesn't help, the many acquaintances, the friends who take days to answer, the meals bought and eaten alone, the life that looks full from outside, nobody noticing when you go quiet, and the early deaths. The need for your own people explains 8, one more: the same ones except the full life, plus the low mood and the falling time together. Helping each other leads because the distance from how humans lived is widest there. Company has not gone: Americans who live alone see friends more than married people do, and the countries with the most one-person homes report the least serious loneliness. What has gone is anyone who depends on you. For nearly all of human history your food, and your care when you were sick, came from people who counted on you in return. Now both come from strangers you pay. The search for a mate explains the missing partner, and the threat system explains the pulling back. None of the four explains why companies sell company. They sell it because lonely people pay.

What cuts against it

  • In 20 randomised trials of ways to reduce loneliness, the programmes that changed how lonely people read others worked best. Adding chances to meet people did almost nothing in the two trials that tried it, and adding support did little in twelve. None of the four kinds of programme in that review was about being needed: they changed how people read others, gave them support, trained their skills or gave them chances to meet people.

What would prove this wrong

If people whom others depend on every day are as lonely as people nobody depends on, this page is wrong.

A study can settle this.

Strongest objection"I'm around people all day and I'm still lonely." Yes. The hunger is for people who know you, need you and would notice if you went quiet. Most colleagues, customers and followers are none of those.
