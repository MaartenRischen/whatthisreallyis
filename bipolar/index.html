What it really is

A strong pull to the top, and nobody around to bring you back down.

Bipolar is what psychiatry calls people whose drive speeds up for days or weeks: little sleep, fast talk, big plans, big spending, and the feeling of standing above everyone else. Then it stops, and a deep low often follows. Every human keeps track of where they stand and goes after a higher place.Some people are born with a stronger pull, and genes set how easily it speeds up and how hard it pushes. For nearly all of human history you lived among a few dozen people who had known you all your life. The dopamine circuit that sends you after things is 460 million years old. People have always differed in how strong both are. Humans have existed for 300,000 years, and for nearly all of that time they lived in camps of a few dozen people with no rulers. Anyone who bragged or tried to give orders was teased, then avoided, and thrown out if they kept going. Then people started farming and settling down, and villages grew into towns and cities. That began 12,000 years ago. Now you can chase a goal for months without anyone who knows you seeing you do it, and you are ranked against millions of strangers. So the pull is hundreds of millions of years old, and having nobody to bring it down is new.

How it happens

  1. 1

    Every human goes after a higher place.

    The science says

    Every human keeps track of where they stand and goes after a higher place.

    Fish, reptiles, birds and mammals all have a rank system. In people it weighs most what the people who know you think: across 15 small-scale communities, the shame people expected over an act tracked how their own community would see it. The drive that sends you after food, a mate and a better place is found in every animal with a backbone.

    The science says

    Some people are born with a stronger pull, and it runs in families.

    In Swedish records on nine million people, children of a parent with the diagnosis got it about six times as often as others. People who have frequent mild highs want power, fame and money more than others do, also when they are well. Having a stronger pull than most was useful for nearly all of human history, and it shows in people who carry some of it without the full highs: the twins of people with the diagnosis are more cheerful and better with words than others, and close relatives more often work in science and the arts.

    A big win, a lost night or an antidepressant speeds it up.

    In two studies, reaching a big goal, such as a new job or a place at graduate school, raised manic symptoms over the next two to three months. A lottery win did not. The cost-benefit analysis

Your brain does a cost-benefit analysis on every chance to rise: the effort and the risk against what a higher place brings in. After a win the next push looks cheap, because the last one paid. In a diary study, people with the diagnosis eased off a goal less than others after it went unexpectedly well. For nearly all of human history the people around you set the price. Anyone who acted bigger than the rest was mocked, avoided and in the end thrown out, so pushing past your place cost you the people you lived with, and the brain eased off early. Now nobody who knows you sets that price. A win can be any size and there is always a bigger one, so the push stays cheap until the money or the body pays for it.

Real example cases

I tried to explain that I was an untapped genius

Sarah Jane Holland, Stylist, 24 April 2023

She was 22. In the year before, she had moved back home after university, been demoted and then let go from a data entry job, and felt worthless. Then she got an offer from her dream university that she could not pay for. She stopped sleeping, wrote down her life goals through the night and made friends with strangers on a trip. Within weeks she was in hospital. “We're not educated to spot mania,” she writes.

I'll pay! I'll pay for everything!

April Kelley, New Zealand Herald, from The Telegraph, 11 February 2025

A film producer in London, diagnosed at 27. Before that she was put on Prozac, which she says led to this kind of carnage. In a high she buys every stranger in the bar a shot and her friends festival tickets, to buy their love, she writes. Her business partner is the first to know when she is heading up, sometimes before she is. On three drugs a day she still has six to ten episodes a year.

So now what

  1. Ask a doctor to check for a physical cause first: an overactive thyroid, or a drug you take. Antidepressants and steroids can set off a high.
  2. Write down what the start of a high looks like in you: less sleep, faster talk, bigger plans. People who learned their own early signs relapsed later and went into hospital a third less often.
  3. Give that list to two or three people who see you every week, and agree now what they do when they see it. Therapy that brought in the family did best.
  4. Arrange your week so people who know you see you most days: a shared house, a team, a fixed meal.
  5. After a big win, make no new plans and no big purchases for a month, and tell your people why.
  6. Keep your sleep at the same hours. For one person in five, lost sleep has set off a high.

If you take lithium or another drug for this: where no physical defect is the cause, the aim is to replace the drug with the real thing: people who know you, see you most days and tell you when you are speeding up. The drug goes as they come in. Bring the dose down with a doctor while you build that. It doesn't have to be the doctor who prescribed it. Never stop suddenly. After a sudden stop of lithium, episodes come back sooner. If you are thinking about ending your life, tell someone today and call your local emergency number.

Why it wins. Rank explains 5 of the 14 details: the feeling of standing above everyone, the law student who misses being special, better, worse or broken, the high that came after an offer from a dream university and after losing a job, and the low that follows. The drive to go after things explains 3: the energy and the big plans, the high after the win, and the night spent writing life goals. People close enough to see it start explain 3: the partner who sees it first, the rounds bought to buy love, and nobody around knowing what a high looks like. The body clock explains the lost sleep. Rank leads because it explains the most, and because the gap is the widest: a camp of a few dozen brought down anyone who acted bigger, and today a person is ranked against millions and nobody brings them down. Four details are explained by none of them: that it runs in families, which sets how strong the pull is; the antidepressant that set it off; the missing test; and the drugs that leave life empty while the highs keep coming.

What backs it

  • Striving sets it off, and luck does not. 43 people with the diagnosis were rated every month. The bigger a goal they had reached, such as a new job or a poetry award, the higher their manic symptoms over the next two months. A lottery win or a tax refund made no difference.

    1 source: Johnson et al. 2000. Full listClosetested Johnson SL, Sandrow D, Meyer B and others (2000). Increases in manic symptoms after life events involving goal attainment. Journal of Abnormal Psychology 109.

  • The people around you can catch it. Across six trials with 690 people, those taught to spot their own early signs went longer before the next episode and went into hospital a third less often.

    1 source: Morriss et al. 2007. Full listClosetested many times Morriss RK, Faizal MA, Jones AP, Williamson PR, Bolton C, McCarthy JP (2007). Interventions for helping people recognise early signs of recurrence in bipolar disorder. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2010.

  • Family counts most. Across 39 trials, therapy that brought in the family or partner cut the odds of a new episode most of all the add-on treatments.

    1 source: Miklowitz et al. 2021. Full listClosetested many times Miklowitz DJ, Efthimiou O, Furukawa TA and others (2021). Adjunctive psychotherapy for bipolar disorder: a systematic review and component network meta-analysis. JAMA Psychiatry 78.

What cuts against it

  • In lab tasks, people with the diagnosis work less for a reward. Across 68 studies in which people chose whether to work harder for more money, people with the diagnosis chose the harder work less often than others. People at risk of it who had not been diagnosed did not differ.

    1 source: Pillny et al. 2026. Full listClosetested many times Pillny M, Renz KE, Hay A and others (2026). Effort-based decision making in psychopathology: a transdiagnostic multilevel meta-analysis and systematic review of behavioral patterns and mechanisms underlying amotivational psychopathology. Psychological Bulletin 152.

  • The genes behind the diagnosis brought no extra children. Among 93,720 Icelanders, a gene score for bipolar had no link with how many children people had, and the top 1 percent of the score had fewer.

    1 source: Mullins et al. 2017. Full listClosetested Mullins N, Ingason A, Porter H and others (2017). Reproductive fitness and genetic risk of psychiatric disorders in the general population. Nature Communications 8.

What would prove this wrong

If people with the diagnosis who live among people who know them, see them most days and tell them when they speed up have as many full highs as people who live alone, this page is wrong.

A study can settle this.

Strongest objection“My uncle's highs put him in hospital. That is an illness.” A high can wreck a life and still have no defect behind it. Dangerous and diseased are two different things. Check for a physical cause first, and never stop a drug suddenly.
