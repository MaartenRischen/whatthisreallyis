Midterm anxiety

Election anxiety is what a brain built to bring down bosses feels when it can't. For most of human history there were no rulers: anyone who tried to dominate was mocked, shunned or driven out by everyone acting together, and people could always walk to another camp. A midterm hands power to strangers you can't check or leave, pools your vote among millions, and seats the other side at your family's table.

Source: Election Anxiety: 70% Stressed About 2026 Midterms, LifeStance Health

The alarm fires because the coming winner will rule you and you have no way to check them. For most of human history, decisions that affected the group were reached by talking toward agreement, a leader's pull lasted only while people chose to follow, and anyone could move to another camp. A midterm fills seats with strangers almost no voter has met, locks them in for fixed terms, pools your vote among tens of millions, and leaves you in a country you can't walk out of. Among people who discuss politics with a clinician, 18% talk about helplessness or lack of control, which is the control system reporting that its own action barely moves the result.

The other side now sits at your own table. In forager bands the line between us and them kept moving: most campmates were not close kin, camps split and re-formed every few weeks, and people lived alongside those they picked. Today the opposing coalition is tens of millions of fellow citizens on a fixed team, and some of them are your parents, siblings and in-laws. That is why 37% of Americans dread political talk at family gatherings and 31% would rather wash all the dishes. A Republican and a Democrat at the same dinner run the same alarm, and each reads the other's win as a would-be boss taking over the group they both belong to.

Nothing ends the threat, so the alarm stays on. For most of human history an alarm, true or false, was followed by an action and then calm, often with the return to the group. Election talk arrives through phones and feeds all day for months, the count and its aftermath can stay unsettled for days or weeks, and the winners then hold power for years with the next campaign close behind. The survey finds 42% anxious about the future and 25% feeling hopelessness or doom, which is what a threat system reports when no all-clear is coming.

The youngest adults carry the most stress and the fewest people to carry it with. Ancestral events were talked over face to face, with a small circle of confidants inside a camp of a few dozen familiar people, and trusted company in the room is what turns the threat system down. In this survey 55% of Gen Z report moderate or significant stress, the highest of any generation, and 41% of Gen Z have talked election stress through with an AI chatbot, against 6% of boomers. A chatbot answers back with no person behind it, so the alarm gets none of the input that settles it.

The headline number is also softer than it looks. The 70% counts anyone with at least some stress, while the share with a moderate or significant amount is 43%. LifeStance, which commissioned the survey and sells therapy appointments, calls the whole thing election anxiety and offers a plan for election night, while the brain underneath is reading a fight over who rules it that it can't stop, leave or end.

So now what?

  • Watch the results in one room with the few people you trust most, and talk it over with them through the evening and the days after.
  • Put political effort into a group small enough that people know you by name and you can see what your work changes: a school board meeting, a tenants' group, a local campaign run by neighbours.
  • Turn off news alerts and check the count at one set time a day, so the threat cue arrives in an episode with a gap after it.

What cuts against it

  • Late Pleistocene social life was probably more varied than the mobile egalitarian model, with some forager groups showing inequality and seasonal hierarchy, and the egalitarian model's own source notes that an occasional intimidator could tyrannize a band for a time before the group removed him. Free exit between camps comes from immediate-return foragers.

  • Many ancestral dangers had no quick all-clear: about 49 percent of forager children died before 15 and illness caused most deaths in small-scale societies. This limits claims about brief, escapable threats, and leaves the no-check, no-exit, no-influence reading standing.
