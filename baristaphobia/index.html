Baristaphobia

Baristaphobia is what a healthy human brain does when it has to ask a stranger for food. For most of human history a stranger could get you killed and food came from people you knew, and now you buy it out loud from someone nobody can vouch for while more strangers watch.

Source: Some Gen Zers suffer from ‘Baristaphobia,’ and fear ordering in coffee shops, The Independent, 30 Jul 2025

The barista is exactly the face the human alarm was built to catch: someone nobody you know can vouch for. For most of human history almost every face a person met was already known, or could be traced through allies, within a lifetime pool of more than a thousand people. A genuine stranger was rare, and the alarm met one fast, before slow thought could weigh whether this one was friendly. A coffee counter turns that rare event into an ordinary errand, with a face tied to nobody you know, and the brain scores it the way it scored a stranger walking into camp.

The alarm fires at a harmless barista because it was built to fire at the faintest hint. Missing one real danger could kill you, while running from a false alarm cost a little energy, so most of its firings were false even when it worked perfectly. That is why one young person in the article calls ordering 'so anxiety provoking idk why': the threat circuit scores the stranger before deliberate thought gets a chance to say it is only someone making coffee.

The counter is faced alone, and it ends with no all-clear. For most of human history an alarm stood down once the danger passed, and the return to calm came through your own people: getting back to the group and being close to them. At the counter nobody of yours stands beside you, and you walk off alone with nobody to confirm it went fine. The app sharpens this in one specific way, because it removes the stranger every time, so the alarm is relieved by avoidance and never meets a stranger who turns out to be harmless.

The queue adds a second alarm, the fear of being judged, and it fires at watchers who keep no record of you. In a camp of a few dozen people, everyone who saw what you did knew your name and your history, their talk about you shaped who would back you, and you could answer them or move to another camp and live a slip down. A queue of strangers will never see you again and cannot be answered, yet 38 percent of Gen Z in the survey dread making coffee for someone else because they fear the social consequences of a bad cup.

The survey's own numbers shrink its headline. The 'nearly half' refers to the 47 percent who say they don't set foot in coffee shops, while only 7 percent admit to being too scared to approach a barista at all. The survey comes from Philips, which sells espresso machines and closes the story by pitching them as a way to get 'full control' at home.

So now what?

  • Go to the counter with a friend the first few times, so someone of your own stands beside you while you order.
  • Keep going back to the same cafe at the same time of day, so the person behind the counter becomes a familiar face who knows your order.
  • Order in person at quiet hours when no queue is listening, and save the busy rush for the app.
