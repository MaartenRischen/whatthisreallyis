What really is:

Celebrity

What it really is

A camp bond that runs one way.

In a forager camp, the few dozen people you saw and heard every day were people who knew you back. Screens now bring a face into your day that never sees you, and the famous get thousands who know them and almost none they know.

Then and now

Hadza camps averaged about 30 people, and nearly everyone a forager met was already known or one or two links away.

A fan hears a star talk every day on a phone, and the star never hears the fan.

Sources: E603; EEA-SOC-02. Time scale: Hublin et al. 2017; Richter et al. 2017.Gold rings: the alarm is on

How it happens

  1. 1

    In a forager camp you lived among a few dozen people. Almost every face you saw belonged to someone you knew, and who knew you.

    The science says

    Hadza camps average about 30 people (E603). Cor's camp-size baseline puts a camp at roughly 25 to 50 adults. From a known network capped near 150, Cor argues that a face nobody knew was rare.

    Screens bring one face into your day, every day, talking as if to you. That face never sees you.

  3. so3

    The bonding system responds. Fans feel friendship, first love or family, copy the star and defend them, and they grieve when the star dies.

    The science says

    The bonding system brings pain-grade distress when people are gone. The separation-distress circuits are similar across mammals and some birds (E9). In young animals, opioids lower that distress (E269).

    The science says

What they say it isWhat it really is

parasocial relationship

A bond that runs one way, to a face you see and hear every day that never sees you. Cor ranks it near the bottom of what the bonding system can live on.

celebrity worship

The same pull, running harder. In one student sample it went with feeling similar to the star and with insecurity, and the study could not tell which came first.

fandom

Fans bonding with each other through a shared star. This part has real people in it who see you back.

the loneliness that comes with fame

Thousands of people know you and you know almost none of them. The few close ties that steady a person are no easier to get.

Which system wins

  1. 1

    Oldest. Brainstem and hypothalamus, about 500 million years old

    Energy regulation holds total daily spending in a narrow band, and one Cor record argues that the brain counts trusted people nearby as resources, the way it counts glucose (E124).

    A stranger who acts as if they know you is a threat cue for the star, so the approach is draining and strange.A one-way bond delivers the feeling of belonging and a friend for almost no effort and no risk of rejection, so people take it.The mating system treats a famous face as a mate or first love, and fame makes real partnering hard for the star.

  2. 2

    Limbic system, about 200 million years old

    The bonding system attaches to people and brings pain-grade distress when they are gone, and here it fires for a star who never responds.

    The bonding system treats a person seen and heard this often as one of your people, so fans feel family, friendship or first love for someone who does not know they exist. WinsFans copy the people they rank high or feel similar to, and the crowd levels anyone who seems too powerful.

  3. 3

    Newest. Cortex, about 50 to 100 million years old, with human add-ons from the last few million

    Status monitoring tracks where you stand against others in a known group, and a star's huge audience falls far outside that group.

    The alliance system gives fans an ally to defend and a group to belong to, and turns on a figure judged to have broken the rules or be too powerful.

Simplified. The older the system, the harder its signal hits and the less it can learn that the people on a screen are not in the room.

The 12 details
  1. People feel a close, personal, friend-like or family-like bond with a famous person who does not know they exist.
  2. Famous people feel isolated and lonely, as if nobody really understands them.
  3. Strangers approach famous people as though they know them and their private lives, which the famous find draining and strange.
  4. Fans imitate the celebrities they admire or feel similar to, picking up their style, phrases and mannerisms.
  5. Fans defend the celebrities they like as if they were friends, and turn on them when the celebrity does something that does not fit the image fans hold.
  6. When a celebrity is seen as too powerful or too famous, the public quickly reframes them as difficult or arrogant and believes the worst before knowing the details; the article describes this happening especially to women.
  7. When a celebrity dies, fans grieve as if they had lost a family member or part of their own childhood, and gather together at vigils to mourn.
  8. Fans bond with each other through a shared star or show and form group identities, such as 'directioners' or the 'Bravo universe'.
  9. People with lower self-esteem, or a more insecure and emotionally sensitive self-focus, form stronger and more extreme attachments to celebrities.
  10. The attachment comes through screens, posters, TV and social media, which give on-demand glimpses into the daily lives of people the fan never meets.
  11. Strong celebrity attachment goes with placing more importance on money, status and possessions, and is fed by TV channels, influencer accounts and online campaigns; in one controversy over 23% of the posting and comments were generated by bots.
  12. Fame strains famous people's romantic relationships, and they find it hard to find a partner who can handle the attention that comes with them.

This phenomenon in Cor 4 systems, each with its grade and age

M5Status Monitoring

Cor grade: established, circuit about 50-100 Myr old

In plain EnglishConstantly monitors where you stand relative to others.

The researchRank regulation. Shame, pride, submission, defeat, rank vigilance. Not optional - runs continuously.

Fully met byAcceptable position in a bounded, personally-known reference group on the order of ~150 (an illustrative central tendency, not a hard number; the precise figure is contested per Lindenfors 2021). Earned prestige preferred over coerced dominance (Henrich & Gil-White distinction - needs explicit treatment).

M5 in Cor: every source and grade

M3Social Bonding

Cor grade: established, circuit about 200 Myr old wins here

In plain EnglishForms bonds with other humans. Pain-grade distress when they're gone.

The researchAttachment and co-regulation system. Includes grief mode (absorbed from old M9 GRIEF). Two states: security (enables broadly) and grief (suppresses broadly).

Fully met byReliable human co-regulation through physical presence, operating through nested social layers: ~5 intimate co-regulators (primary emotional/physical support), ~15 sympathy group, ~50 close social partners, ~150 coherent face-to-face relationships (Dunbar number - outer cognitive ceiling). Each layer represents qualitatively different relationship types with different obligations and investment requirements. The innermost ~5 provide the majority of social regulation; the critical variable at this layer is reliability, not count. The ~150 outer figure is an illustrative central tendency, not a hard limit: its derivation from the primate neocortex-ratio regression is contested (Lindenfors, Wartel & Lind 2021). What the evidence carries is the nested layering and the reliability-over-count point, not the exact rung figures; the layered structure and its low-hundreds clustering are separately observed in phone, Christmas-card, and Facebook data.

M3 in Cor: every source and grade

M11Cooperation / Alliance

Cor grade: strongly supported, circuit about 2-5 Myr old

In plain EnglishReciprocal exchange, cheater detection, coalitional identity.

The researchReciprocity + norm enforcement + alliance dynamics. Absorbs old M10 (Cooperation) and M11 (Coalitional). NOTE: Tier 2 is a confidence grade, not an entailment status. M11 IS entailment-forced - convergence C11 forces it - and its four converging streams (Fehr & Gächter, Boehm, Tomasello, Trivers) strongly support it without reaching the top confidence tier.

Fully met byTrusted reciprocal network with norm enforcement within a group small enough for direct reputation tracking (on the order of ~150 - the same low-hundreds ceiling that bounds M3; the precise figure is contested per Lindenfors 2021). Norm enforcement requires repeated direct interaction where defectors can be identified, remembered, and sanctioned. Beyond that ceiling, cooperation requires institutional scaffolding (formal rules, third-party enforcement, shared fictions) rather than direct reciprocity. No shared fiction at the direct-reciprocity layer.

What cuts against it

  • One study links strong celebrity attachment to insecurity, materialism and feeling similar to the star, which points to differences between people as well as to the setting.

  • Fans meet, sing at vigils and form groups like directioners, so part of fandom is a two-way bond between fans.

  • Michael B. Jordan names the weight of responsibility as isolating, which may drive the loneliness of fame as much as the size of the crowd does.

Not in Cor yet

  • Seeing and hearing a face often is enough to switch on attachment.

    Study: A study measuring attachment against daily exposure to a media figure, building on Horton and Wohl 1956.

  • Grief for a dead celebrity runs on the same separation-distress system as grief for kin.

    Study: A physiology or imaging study of fans after a star's death, compared with people grieving a relative.

  • Famous people have fewer close inner-circle ties than other people.

    Study: A network-layer survey of public figures using the layer counts in Sutcliffe et al. 2012.

So now what

  1. Move one hour a week from a star's feed to seeing one person who knows you back.
  2. Compare yourself with the people you know face to face, and leave stars out of the count.
  3. Go to the gig, the vigil or the watch party with fans you can meet, so the shared star brings you people who see you.

What would prove this wrong

If fans who see and hear a star daily form no stronger attachment than fans who see that star rarely, the lead reading is wrong.

Cor marks the figure of about 150 relationships as contested (E25). The nested layers of closeness hold up, and the exact number does not.

Strongest objectionFans know the star does not know them. Following a star may be entertainment and taste, with the bonding system playing no part.
