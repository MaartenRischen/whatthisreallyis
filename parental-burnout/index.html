What really is:

Parental burnout

What it really is

A camp's job on one or two adults.

For most of human history raising a child took a whole camp. The body is right that it's too much, and when the help isn't there it pulls back, from the work and from the child.

Then and now

For 300,000 years, parents had help.

Feeling you can’t do this alone is completely normal.

Sources: EEA-CARE-01; Hrdy 2009. Time scale: Hublin et al. 2017; Richter et al. 2017.Gold rings: the alarm is on

How it happens

  1. 1

    A human child costs more than two adults can supply.

    The science says

    Raising a human to adulthood takes about 13 million calories, more than a mother could supply alone. Other ape mothers raise their young alone. Human mothers never did.

    argued, not yet testedE23 Hrdyargued, not yet testedE401 HrdyM9 Care and alloparenting, established

  2. so2

    For most of human history the help was a few steps away: grandmothers, aunts, older kids and other mothers.

    The science says

    Teams working with different forager peoples all find babies held, fed and watched by many people besides the mother. Among the Hadza, others hold the baby about a third of the time. Agta children have about nine carers who are not even kin.

    tested many timesEEA-CARE-01 A village, not a pairtested onceE821 Page et al. 2021, one population

  3. so3

    Now the help is gone: one or two adults, family in another city, both out at work.

    The science says

    The household of a couple and their children, on its own, is about a century old. Grandparents now often live far away. Whether grandmothers raised child survival in the past is disputed.

    argued, not yet testedE403 HrdycontestedEEA-CARE-04 Grandparents as helpers

    Not in Cor yet: across 42 countries, parents burn out most where people live most for themselves.

  4. so4

    The body reads that correctly: exhaustion, a short fuse, stress hormones up.

    The science says

    Stress that never gets resolved builds up as wear across the body: heart, immune system, metabolism, memory.

  5. so5

    Then it does what mammal parents do when help runs out: it pulls back. Feeling nothing for your child is the body cutting what it can't carry.

    The science says

    Human mothers set how much they put into a child by the help around them. Mixed feelings toward a newborn are part of the design. Help changes whether a next child comes at all.

Why it wins. The missing help explains 9 of the 11 details and sits on reproduction: whether the child gets raised. The pulling back runs on an older system, but it only switches on because the help is gone.

The 11 details
  1. Parents are exhausted.
  2. Most do it with no other adult around for much of the day.
  3. Babies feed and wake through the night.
  4. Parents feel distant from their child, or nothing at all.
  5. Guilt about not doing enough.
  6. A short fuse with the kids and each other.
  7. Stress hormones are raised.
  8. It is far more common in individualistic countries.
  9. Culture predicts it better than the number of kids or hours with them.
  10. Work takes one or both parents out of the home all day.
  11. Parents measure themselves against other parents and experts.

M9Care / Alloparenting

Cor grade: established, circuit about 200 Myr old wins here

In plain EnglishDistributed care. The organism expects a village, not solo parenting.

The researchParental and alloparental care system, run on oxytocin and prolactin. Cor's own prediction for a family with no network around it: a parent burnout cascade.

Fully met byA care network beyond the parents, about four or more people in traditional societies. What counts is that they reliably show up and actually help. Being related matters less.

What cuts against it

  • Individualistic countries also push the perfect-parent standard. The burnout could come from that pressure as much as from missing hands. The 42-country authors lean that way.

  • Parents who pay for daycare and nannies still burn out. Cor's answer is that rotating paid carers are thinner help than the same people every week, and that answer is argued, not tested.

    argued, not yet testedEEA-CARE-02 Carers who are reliably there

  • Help from relatives does not lift child survival in every population studied, and which relatives matter is disputed.

    contestedE703 Sear and Mace 2008, 45 studies

  • Parental burnout is far more common in individualistic countries, more than income, number of kids or hours with them explain.

So now what

  1. Get the same people on fixed days: a grandparent, neighbour or friend who takes the kids at the same time every week.
  2. Team up with one or two other families. Shared dinners, swapped afternoons, kids in a pack.
  3. Live near the help. If family is a flight away, moving closer is a real option.

What would prove this wrong

If parents who get steady hands-on help from the same people every week burn out as often as parents doing it alone, this page is wrong.

Cor holds no direct test yet. The closest: across 42 countries, parents burn out most where people live most for themselves (Roskam et al. 2021, not in Cor yet).
