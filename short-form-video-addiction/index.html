What it really is

Stories about people, all night, from strangers. For nearly all of human history the night's stories came from the people sitting with you, about people you knew. Those stories told you whom you could count on, and the best tellers had more children.

Short-form video addiction is the name for scrolling TikTok, Reels or Shorts for hours and not being able to stop. What people watch on it is other people: their days, their stories, their fights, their jokes. An addictionThe writers themselves and a Yale psychiatrist quoted in The Washington Post: the “dopamine hit”, a brain that reacts as it does to money and food.No test finds a defect in a person who can't stop scrolling. The system that fires is the one that wants what a human needs, and one of the things a human needs is news of other people.     Humans want news of other people.

    The science says

    Humans are built to want news of other people, because knowing who did what decided whom you could count on.

    Relaxed conversations overheard in public places are mostly about people: relationships and personal experience. Forager bands kept track of every member, good points and bad, and everyone knew the talk could turn to them. In experiments, people passed on news about selfish players even when it cost them, and the threat of being talked about made others more generous.     The next one costs a swipe.

    The science says

    Your brain leaves a source when it pays less than the others, and the next video costs one swipe.

    In a foraging game played for money, people timed their leaving of a tree by the rule foraging theory predicts: go when one more harvest pays less than the average of the whole orchard. They stayed a little too long. Batek foragers moved camp when what the camp gave fell to the average of the forest around it. In a feed the next source is one swipe away, so the brain moves on from a dull video in seconds and has no reason to leave the feed. Heavy users in The Washington Post's data swiped faster as the months went on.

The cost-benefit analysis

Your brain does a cost-benefit analysis on where to spend the evening. For nearly all of human history the night's stories cost nothing but sitting there: they were told by the fire, by the people you lived with, about people you knew. Today the people who would tell them live across town, and hearing their news takes a message, a trip and the chance that they are busy. The feed puts the next story in front of you for one swipe. The brain leaves a source when it pays less than the others, and when the next one costs a swipe it moves on to the next video in seconds and never leaves the feed. What changed is the price of the real route. Friends see each other less, and a company made the stand-in cost nothing. The hours come out of the night.

He was a junior at a demanding high school, in bed at 2:30 a.m. with school in a few hours. He had downloaded TikTok in middle school to share funny videos. When the lockdown came, school went online and he stopped playing basketball with his friends, and his use went up to six hours a day. Next So now what

So now what

  1. Spend evenings where people talk: a housemate's kitchen, a friend's place, the same table with the same people every week. The stories there are about people you know, and the people telling them know you.
  2. Tell yours. Call or meet one person tonight, tell them what happened to you today, and ask what happened to them.
  3. When a friend sends a reel, answer the friend. The video stands in for a story told by someone who knows you. The friend who sent it is real, so say what it made you think of, or ask to meet.
  4. Watch together when you watch. A video on one screen with your housemates, laughing at the same thing, is time with them. The video is the stand-in and the people are real.
  5. Quit with your friends. Ask your group chat to delete the app on the same day. Leaving alone means missing the joke, and when the whole group leaves nobody misses it.
  6. Join something that meets every week with the same people: a team, a choir, a class.
  7. Charge the phone outside the bedroom, so the night goes to sleep.

Send this to the friend who sends you twenty reels a day.

What backs it

  • Talk among people is mostly about people. Observers listening to relaxed conversations in public found them mostly about social matters: personal relationships and experiences.

    1 source: Dunbar et al. 1997. Full listClosetested many times Dunbar RIM, Marriott A, Duncan NDC (1997). Human conversational behavior. Human Nature 8.

  • Being cut off from people raises the wanting. After ten hours alone, adults' midbrain reward area reacted to pictures of their favourite social activities the way it reacted to food pictures after a fast.

    1 source: Tomova et al. 2020. Full listClosetested Tomova L, Wang KL, Thompson T and others (2020). Acute social isolation evokes midbrain craving responses similar to hunger. Nature Neuroscience 23.

  • A one-way bond helps mood and not loneliness. In a preregistered experiment, thinking about a favourite media figure lowered negative mood about as much as thinking about a friend did, and it did not lower loneliness, which thinking about a friend did.

    1 source: Stein et al. 2024. Full listClosetested Stein JP, Liebers N, Faiss M (2024). Feeling Better...But Also Less Lonely? An Experimental Comparison of How Parasocial and Social Relationships Affect People's Well-Being. Mass Communication and Society 27.

  • Taking a feed away frees hours for people. US Facebook users paid to deactivate it for four weeks gained about an hour a day, spent more time with friends and family, and reported a small rise in well-being.

    1 source: Allcott et al. 2020. Full listClosetested Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2020). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. American Economic Review 110.

What cuts against it

  • Loneliness alone does not explain who watches. In a survey of 838 people, social isolation did not predict interest in celebrity gossip, and among US teenagers those who spent more time with friends in person also used social media more. So the page cannot say people scroll because they have nobody. What it rests on is the pull every human has to hear about people, and a feed that never runs out of them.

What would prove this wrong

If a feed of the same speed with no people in it, only landscapes, objects or shapes, holds viewers as long as a feed of people, this page is wrong.

A study can settle this.

Strongest objection“People watched TV for hours too.” They did, and the same pull was at work: shows about people. A broadcast ended. A feed has no end, and it picks the next video for you from what held you last time.
