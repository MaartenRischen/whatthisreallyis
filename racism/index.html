Every decode, every page and every Decode Talk conversation is made from it. AI compiles the studies into Cor and uses Cor to do the decoding, more objectively than any human can.

The three checks, for every finding in Cor:

  1. Every finding is checked against the study's full text or abstract. Summaries, search snippets and Wikipedia don't count.
  2. The finding says no more than the study does.
  3. Every grade names the strongest study behind it and that study's main weakness.

A grade never goes up because it would help a page. A finding that failed to replicate is marked down, however widely it is cited. Cor keeps records of the evidence against itself, publishes the strongest attacks on its own work and names the results that would kill it.

Monkeys and apes do it. Humans have existed for 300,000 years. For nearly all of that time they lived in camps of a few dozen, inside a wider group of a few hundred to a few thousand people who shared a language and married each other. The group next door spoke differently and looked the same. Some neighbours were visited and married, and some were raided. Then people started farming, and villages grew into towns, cities and states. That began 12,000 years ago. Ships and empires then put whole populations who look different in the same place, with one ruling or owning the other. On that scale it is about 500 years old. So picking sides is at least 25 million years old, and sorting whole populations by colour is about 500 years old.

The cost-benefit analysis

Your brain does a cost-benefit analysis on every person it can't place. Finding out whose side someone is on costs time with them. A sign you can see costs a glance. For nearly all of human history you knew the people around you, so it rarely had to guess. Among millions of strangers there is no time to know each one, so the brain goes by the cheapest sign that works, and colour is the easiest thing to see. It works today because streets, schools, jobs and parties are sorted by it. Show people two teams that cut across colour, and within four minutes they sort the players by team.

So now what

  1. Join something where you need the others and they need you: a team, a crew, a band, a building job. Mixed by colour where you can.
  2. Go every week, with the same people, for months. One afternoon does nothing.
  3. Learn names. A person you know gets judged as that person.
  4. If you hire, cover the name on the CV before you read it.
  5. Stop fighting about race with strangers online. That is picking sides too, and it changes nobody.

Don't book the workshop. Give a mixed team a job that none of them can do alone.

    1 source: Pietraszewski et al. 2014. Full listClosetested Pietraszewski D, Cosmides L, Tooby J (2014). The content of our cooperation, not the color of our skin: an alliance detection system regulates categorization by coalition and race, but not sex. PLoS ONE 9.

What cuts against it

  • Race did not vanish. In the larger repeat of the shirt experiment, sorting by race dropped by less than in the original and was still there.

    1 source: Voorspoels et al. 2014. Full listClosetested many times Voorspoels W, Bartlema A, Vanpaemel W (2014). Can race really be erased? A pre-registered replication study. Frontiers in Psychology 5.

  • Being teammates changed little outside the team. In Iraq, Christians put on football teams with Muslims treated their Muslim teammates better and behaved as before toward other Muslims.

    1 source: Mousa 2020. Full listClosetested Mousa S (2020). Building social cohesion between Christians and Muslims through soccer in post-ISIS Iraq. Science 369.

  • Contact works less well for race. Across randomised studies, contact lowered prejudice about race or ethnicity by less than other prejudice, and none of those studies included anyone over 25.

    1 source: Paluck et al. 2019. Full listClosetested many times Paluck EL, Green SA, Green DP (2019). The contact hypothesis re-evaluated. Behavioural Public Policy 3.

What would prove this wrong

If mixed teams whose members need each other for a year still sort and treat each other by race as much as before, this page is wrong.
