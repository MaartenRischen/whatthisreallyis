What really is:

Imposter syndrome

What it really is

A standing check in a new group.

In a camp of a few dozen adults, the people around you saw what you did and talked about it, and your standing rested on that record. A new job or career change puts you among people who have not seen your work, and the system that tracks standing may read that as unsettled.

How it happens

  1. 1

    In a camp of a few dozen adults, the people around you saw or heard about almost everything relevant you did. Your reputation rested on that record, and a leader's influence lasted only while others chose to listen.

    The science says

    Forager camps held a few dozen adults, all known by name. The band kept a running record of what each person did, and gossip spread it. Camps had no rulers, and influence came from prestige that others gave freely.

  2. so2

    A new job or a career change puts you in an assigned group of people who have not seen you work. The proof you built in the old group stays behind.

    The science says

    Today an employer, an address or a platform mostly assigns who you work among, so you do not choose these people. Much of what people do happens among others who do not know their history.

  3. so3

    After a step up, the people around you give vaguer feedback and fewer concrete corrections. The signal of where you stand stays thin.

    The science says

    Modern visibility is mediated, curated and thin. It has lost the close watching by known peers that the reputation system was built to run on.

    Not in Cor yet: how the feedback people receive changes after a promotion. This link rests on the instances.

  4. so4

    Unstable rank can set off the threat system: in one monkey study and one human brain-imaging study it went with amygdala activity, and in the monkeys with anxiety.

    The science says

    In one human brain-imaging study, unstable rank activated the amygdala and stable rank did not, and in one monkey study a rival's threat to rank went with amygdala activity and anxiety. Self-esteem falls on cues that a small set of people who matter value you less. Waiting an unknown time for a shock is aversive, and the dread activates the amygdala.

  5. so5

    The system answers by protecting standing. The person over-prepares, stays quiet in meetings and keeps the feeling to themselves.

    Because the feeling stays hidden, nobody says they feel the same, so the standing stays unsettled.

    The science says

    People act to protect their standing when cues signal they may be valued less. Shame comes from strategies that signal awareness of lost standing and limit the damage. Foragers worried about their peers' opinion and checked their own behaviour accordingly.

Real example cases

Nearly three in five (58 per cent) workers experience imposter syndrome in the workplace - meaning they often believe they are inferior to others or have faked their way into positions despite impressive accomplishments

Jim Wilson, Canadian Lawyer, reporting an Indeed survey of UK workers, May 10, 2022

58 per cent feel it and 94 per cent have not talked about it at work, which fits a standing check that keeps a low reading out of sight.

We found that when students think their class is competitive, they feel more like an imposter on a day-to-day basis and this is most problematic for first-generation college students.

Elizabeth A. Canning, Washington State University, in PsyPost, 11 January 2020

Students who saw their class as competitive reported more of the fear, and the gap for first-generation students was absent in classes they did not see as competitive.

What they say it isWhat it really is

imposter syndrome

The system that tracks standing, reporting that your place is unsettled in a group that has not seen you work.

impostor phenomenon

The same fear of being found out. It spikes at a new job, a promotion or a career change.

impostorism

The fear measured as a score. It rises in rooms that feel competitive and evens out across groups where they do not.

feeling like a fraud

The sense that the group would lower your standing if it saw you clearly, held while it has no record of your work.

self-doubt

A low reading on the gauge of how much the people around you value you. That gauge falls on cues that they value you less.

Simplified. The older the system, the harder its signal hits and the less it can learn that the people on a screen are not in the room.

The 12 details
  1. People believe they have faked their way into their position and fear being found out as less capable than others think, even when they are qualified and accomplished.
  2. People dismiss or downplay their successes as luck, timing or sheer effort rather than ability.
  3. The feeling comes with anxiety, rumination and poor sleep, including nightmares about losing the job and checking that work accounts still work.
  4. People overwork and over-prepare to keep from being exposed, putting in longer hours and doing the old job on top of the new one.
  5. People pull back from visibility: they avoid speaking up or asking questions in meetings, skip applying for internal promotions, and engage and attend less.
  6. People hide the feeling at work, fearing they would be seen as less capable, embarrassed or gossiped about; in one survey 94 per cent had not discussed it at work.
  7. It starts or spikes when people enter a new position, such as a new job, a promotion or a career change, where their old proof of competence no longer applies.
  8. It is stronger in settings that feel competitive and where standing is evaluated, such as competitive classrooms and powerful corporate or academic roles; in non-competitive classes the gap between groups disappeared.
  9. It hits harder those who differ from the norm of the group they joined (first-generation students, transgender workers, women, career changers) and younger workers (27 per cent of 25- to 39-year-olds against three per cent of those 65 or older), and often strikes high achievers.
  10. The people around them rarely see it because performance holds up, and after a step up they give vaguer feedback and fewer concrete corrections.
  11. It eases when people talk with a mentor, manager or peers and find that others they respect have the same fear.
  12. It is sold as a personal condition to overcome through reframing, positive self-talk, celebrating wins, mentorship programs, therapy, support groups and employer mental health support.

What cuts against it

  • People promoted inside teams that have watched them for years also feel it, and the known-group reading explains that less well.

  • The rank-uncertainty finding rests on two single studies, and the link between status and stress depends on how a hierarchy is organised.

    tested onceE341 Sapolsky 2017seen in animalsE562 Sapolsky 2005contestedE340 Sapolsky 2017

  • The surveys count the feeling by self-report, and the classroom study is correlational, so it cannot show whether competition raises the fear or the fear makes a room feel competitive.

Not in Cor yet

  • Imposter feelings fall as the same colleagues watch a person's work over months.

    Study: A longitudinal experience-sampling study of new hires, extending Canning et al. 2020 from classrooms to workplaces, that tracks imposter feelings against time observed by the same colleagues.

  • Feedback gets vaguer and less corrective after a promotion.

    Study: A field study comparing the content of feedback given to the same people before and after promotion.

  • Hearing that respected peers share the fear lowers it.

    Study: A randomised study in which new hires hear, or do not hear, senior colleagues describe the same fear, with imposter scores measured afterwards.

So now what

  1. In your first weeks, do part of your work where the new group can see it, so a record of you starts forming.
  2. Ask one or two colleagues you respect, face to face, whether they felt the same when they started.
  3. Ask your manager for one concrete correction on a set day each week, so you can judge your standing from what they say.

What would prove this wrong

Compare people promoted inside a small team that has watched their work for years with people who start in a new group. If the first group reports the fear as often as the second, this page is wrong.

Cor's record that rank uncertainty, more than low rank, sets off the threat system (E341) rests on one monkey study and one human brain-imaging study, and neither has been repeated. Whether low rank is stressful depends on how the hierarchy is organised (E562).

Strongest objectionPeople promoted inside their own team, who were already doing the job, still feel it, and so do high achievers who have been in a group for years. That points to a trait of the person more than to how new the group is.
