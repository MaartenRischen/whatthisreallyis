The three checks, for every finding in Cor:

  1. Every finding is checked against the study's full text or abstract. Summaries, search snippets and Wikipedia don't count.

What it really is

Being ranked by people who have never seen your work. For nearly all of human history the people who judged you had watched you work for years, and a new job puts you among strangers who have seen none of it.

The cost-benefit analysis

Your brain does a cost-benefit analysis on showing your work to a new group. Among people who have seen nothing of you, one mistake is all they have, so being seen costs a lot. Asking for help costs the same, because it shows what you can't do before they have seen what you can. Hiding the work, over-preparing and asking nobody costs hours and puts nothing at risk today, so the brain picks it. For nearly all of human history the sum came out the other way. The people around you had watched you for years, a mistake was one entry among hundreds, and help was asked for and given back every day. What changed is the cost of being seen. A new job, a new school or a promotion puts you among strangers, often picked for being the best, and each new job starts the count again. The route the brain picks keeps the new group from seeing anything, so your place among them stays unsettled.

Real example cases

I didn’t feel like I could ask for help, because it would expose me as an imposter to everyone else.

Angela Riggs, engineering manager, on her blog, July 2023

She froze near the end of her first 90 days at a big company, after two layoffs and two moves in a few years. Her manager told her to tell the team. She asked them for help, and they were glad to give it: “They wanted me to succeed.” Her new colleagues said they had felt the same when they started.

my successes actually made me feel more ashamed, because they meant that I was fooling more and more people in increasingly high-stakes roles

Luisa Rodriguez, researcher, 80,000 Hours, April 2022

Each new role put her in front of more people who had seen little of her work. She worked long hours without telling her colleagues and held drafts back from her boss for months. With her therapist she set herself tasks: show early drafts to her manager, ask for criticism, say how long the work took. Each one showed her what she was good at and what she was not.

It doesn't go away, that feeling that you shouldn't take me that seriously.

Michelle Obama, to 300 schoolgirls in north London, December 2018, reported by CBS News

She had been asked how it felt to be seen as a symbol of hope. Millions of people know her name, and almost none of them have worked with her. Of the people at the most powerful tables she had sat at, she said: “they are not that smart.”

it had nothing to do with the job itself or how anyone treated me

Nadya Primak, software engineer, DEV Community, September 2019

She wrote it after her first week in a new job, having had a lot of different jobs in four years. Earlier, as a woman who came to code from design, she felt those two things “were counted against me the second I walked through the door”.

Next So now what

So now what

  1. Show your work before it is finished, to the people you work with: a draft, a half-made plan, how long it took you. Your record with them starts when they see it.
  2. Ask someone in the new group for help with something real, and help them back when they need it. That is how strangers become people who know you.
  3. Keep the people who already know your work. When you start somewhere new, stay in touch with an old colleague who saw what you did.
  4. Stay long enough to be known. Each new job starts the record at zero.
  5. A list of your achievements, written alone, is a stand-in for what the people around you think of your work. Use it to get yourself to speak up, then show them the work.

Why it wins. Your standing among people who have seen none of your work explains 10 of the 15 details: where it starts, that nobody in the group caused it, that each success makes it worse, the wins put down to luck, the group picked for being the best, the hidden work, the hours in secret, that almost everyone feels it at the start, that it stays at the top, and the many new groups. The 15 details
  1. It starts on the way into a group: a new job, a new university, a promotion, the first 90 days.
  2. The people around did nothing to cause it: “nothing to do with the job itself or how anyone treated me”.
  3. Each success makes it worse, because it means fooling more people in bigger roles.
  4. Every win is put down to luck, a trick or being liked.
  5. The new group was picked for being the best: a university out of her league, an impressive community, highly technical colleagues.
  6. The work gets hidden: caveats on everything, drafts held back for months, the job she feared avoided.
  7. Long hours, worked in secret, to the edge of burnout.
  8. No asking for help, because it would expose you.
  9. Freezing.
  10. It ended when the new group saw the work and helped: a draft shown to the manager, a team that “wanted me to succeed”.
  11. Almost everyone feels it at the start, and her new colleagues had felt it when they started.
  12. Being counted against at the door: a woman who came to code from design.
  13. It does not go away at the top: a former first lady still has it.
  14. Many new groups in a few years: a lot of different jobs in four years, two layoffs and two moves.
  15. The paid answers: talk therapy, lists of thinking errors, a list of your achievements, a nap and a warm bath, a 2,497-dollar course for coaches.

What would prove this wrong
