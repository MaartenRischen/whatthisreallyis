Cor is the core of this project: an evidence atlas of what humans need, drawn from thousands of studies in evolutionary biology, anthropology, primatology, neuroscience, psychology and psychiatry. Every decode, every page and every Decode Talk conversation is made from it. AI compiles the studies into Cor and uses Cor to do the decoding, more objectively than any human can.

The three checks, for every finding in Cor:

  1. Every finding is checked against the study's full text or abstract. Summaries, search snippets and Wikipedia don't count.
  2. The finding says no more than the study does.
  3. Every grade names the strongest study behind it and that study's main weakness.

A grade never goes up because it would help a page. A finding that failed to replicate is marked down, however widely it is cited. Cor keeps records of the evidence against itself, publishes the strongest attacks on its own work and names the results that would kill it.

What it really is

Someone showing you what you are worth to them. It hurts because that worth decided who fed you and who would have you as a mate.

Rejection is a no from someone whose yes you wanted: a friend, a group, a lover, an employer. The part of you that tracks where you stand is 420 million years old, as old as the first fish with bones.Learn moreClose
Getting attached to particular others is 165 million years old, and every mammal does it. Humans have existed for 300,000 years. For nearly all of that time a person lived in a camp of a few dozen, met about a thousand people in a whole life, and was judged by people who knew them. Families arranged most marriages. Someone who bullied or took without giving was teased and talked about first, and shut out only at the far end. People moved between camps often. Then people started farming, and villages grew into towns and cities. That began 12,000 years ago. Being turned down by hundreds of strangers a year, on a screen, with no reason given, is new. So the alarm is hundreds of millions of years old, and a no with no reason and no way back is new.

The cost-benefit analysis

Your brain does a cost-benefit analysis before you ask anyone for anything. A yes brings in a friend, a place or a mate. A no lowers what you are worth to the people who see it. For nearly all of human history you asked people who knew you. You could judge your chances first, and a no came with a reason you could act on. Now an application or a swipe costs seconds, so people send hundreds, to strangers and software. Asking got cheap. Each no hurts as much as before, and no reason comes with it.

So now what

  1. Ask why. Ask the person, or someone who knows you both. A reason is the only part of a no you can use.
  2. If the reason holds, act on it. Give more, show up, pay back, say sorry. That is how standing gets repaired.
  3. If no reason comes, or they wanted you out of the way, go where you are wanted. Go through people who know you.
  4. Weigh a no by who said it. A filter that read your CV for seconds and a stranger who saw one photo know nothing about you.
  5. Look for work and for dates through people who know you. They put in a word for you, and a no comes back with a reason.
  6. Ask people who know you for something real: help with a move, a lift, an evening.
  7. When you say no to someone, tell them why. You turn people down too, and they are left with the same question.

Don't practise on strangers until it stops hurting. The nos that count come from people who know you. If a no has you thinking about ending your life, tell someone today and call your local emergency number.

What cuts against it

  • A missing reason did not make it hurt more. Among 595 young adults after a break-up they had not wanted, those who were left without a word reported about the same distress as those who were told to their face. Being the one who was left counted for far more than how it was done.

  • The hurt does not wait for someone who knows you. In 120 experiments, people left out of a ball game by a computer program felt far worse within minutes, whatever their age, sex or country.

What would prove this wrong

If people who get a no with its reason from someone who knows them repair nothing more, and feel no better a month later, than people who get silence from a stranger, this page is wrong.

A study can settle this.

Strongest objection"So if people shut me out, I had it coming?" No. The hurt reads what they think of you and never checks whether they are right. Groups shut out people who bully and people who take without giving. People also shut out rivals: in a study of how women compete with other women, gossip and leaving someone out were the main weapons. You can't tell from the inside which one happened to you. Someone who knows you both can.
