The baby bust

The baby bust is a babysitter shortage that no celebrity pep talk can fill. Today a couple raises a child mostly alone, with grandparents far away and friends who rarely bring a meal. Humans are built to hold back on a baby whenever helpers go missing, because for most of our history a child raised without them often died.

Source: Baby Nudges: How Social Influences Shape Fertility Choices, ifstudies.org

Humans evolved to raise children with many hands. For most of human history fathers, grandmothers, older children and unrelated helpers carried, soothed and fed babies. The survey's best countries sit far below that: only around a third expect friends to pitch in, and in Japan just 4 percent expect a meal.

The wish survives the shortage while the commitment drops. In every country studied except Israel, people have fewer children than they say would make them happiest, the drive keeping the wish while reading too few helpers. The drive also tracks sex with a partner who will stay: kin once arranged most marriages and forager adults fed children for years, while today the mate search runs alone, a couple feeds a child from its own pay, and the survey asked only partnered people about intentions.

The report's numbers show where talk runs out. Parental urging goes with 30 percent higher intentions, yet supportive friends go with fewer children ever born, because help peaks at the first baby and fades, where forager children had helpers present for years. Standing once came from prestige among people who knew you, and better hunters had more children; today the admired model is a screen figure, in Japan with one child on average.

Both camps name stated reasons over the same drive. Pronatalists blame lost values and critics blame unequal chores, yet sermons made no detectable difference and housework division predicted nothing, since the drive reads hands and food. For most of human history others held, fed and soothed infants daily, and devout people, who expect far more help from friends, want bigger families.

So now what?

  • Before a baby arrives, line up named people for regular help: who brings meals in the first weeks, who takes a standing weekly afternoon with the child.
  • When choosing where to live, pick a place within easy reach of a grandparent, sibling or close friend who will help with a child week after week.
  • Spend regular time in the homes of friends who are raising young children, so babies and the people caring for them are part of ordinary weeks.

If you take psychiatric medication: where no biomarker shows a physical disease, the goal is to come off it. Work on the real cause, most often your situation with other people, at the same time as a slow taper supervised by your doctor. Never stop suddenly: stopping some drugs at once is dangerous.

What cuts against it

  • Some researchers dispute that humans are cooperative breeders and describe human reproduction as biocultural; a review found most natural-fertility studies do not support a grandmother effect, fathers improved child survival in only a third of studies, and in one forager group mothers still did about 69 percent of infant holding.
