Every decode, every page and every Decode Talk conversation is made from it. AI compiles the studies into Cor and uses Cor to do the decoding, more objectively than any human can.

The three checks, for every finding in Cor:

  1. Every finding is checked against the study's full text or abstract. Summaries, search snippets and Wikipedia don't count.
  2. The finding says no more than the study does.
  3. Every grade names the strongest study behind it and that study's main weakness.

A grade never goes up because it would help a page. A finding that failed to replicate is marked down, however widely it is cited. Cor keeps records of the evidence against itself, publishes the strongest attacks on its own work and names the results that would kill it.

What it really is

One week of living in a camp, bought by people who have none the other 51. The gift economy is made up.

For a week you live in a camp with people who see what you do. You earn your place by building, cooking and fixing. Favours get paid back. People dress up and look for a mate. Humans lived like that for nearly all of their history, so it feels like coming home.The event's ten principles describe something else: gifts with nothing expected back, relying on yourself, everyone welcome. The week runs on the opposite. A gift there buys a favour, a name for your camp or the attention of a pretty stranger. Nobody gets through the desert alone.The organisers grade each camp's standing and hand out tickets by the grade. Then everyone drives home to streets full of strangers, and many crash. Nothing is wrong with them. The week was normal human life, and the other 51 weeks are what's wrong.

The part of you that tracks where you stand is 420 million years old.Learn moreClose
Fish have it. Humans have existed for 300,000 years. For nearly all of that time they lived in camps of a few dozen people they had known for years, and when several bands met, the gathering averaged 165 people. Then people started farming and settling down, and villages grew into towns and cities. That began 12,000 years ago. Now most people live among strangers, and once a year 70,000 of them build a camp in a desert and call it an experiment. It is the oldest way of living there is.

The cost-benefit analysis

Your brain does a cost-benefit analysis on every gift. A gift costs the giver something, and it pays when it buys a favour, a name for your camp or the attention of a pretty stranger. The ten principles say nothing is expected back. The week runs on getting it back. The bigger sum is the week itself. A camp at home costs a meal a week with the same people, all year, and nobody sells it. The desert costs a ticket and a week, and the camp is there when you arrive. The brain takes the week. The camp ends with it, and the fix on sale is next year's ticket.

So now what

  1. Count the people from your camp you have seen since you got home.
  2. Camp with people who live near you, and keep meeting them: one meal a week, the same people.
  3. Build something with them at home. Working where the others can see you is what made the week feel good.
  4. Give where it can come back: to people you will see again.
  5. Earn your standing where you live, from people who know you.

Don't wait for next August. You need people who know you in every week of the year.

Send this to the friend who comes back transformed every September.

Why it wins. Earning standing explains 4 of the 12 details: the grades the organisers give each camp, the virgins at the gate, the hatred of bought camps and the dressing up. Being with your own people explains 3: the camps, the friends people come back for and the crash at home. Trading favours explains 3: the dues, the anger at guests who paid in place of working, and the bigger plate for the pretty girl. Finding a mate explains 2, so it doesn't lead. Four details are explained by none of them: the three rules and the price. The rules describe nothing that happens there, and the price is what the week costs.

The 12 details
  1. The rules say a gift expects nothing back.
  2. The rules say to rely on yourself.
  3. The rules say everyone is welcome and nobody outranks anybody.
  4. A ticket costs 550 to 3,000 dollars, and nearly half the people there earn 100,000 dollars a year or more.
  5. Three in four camp in a group, and camps collect dues.
  6. The organisers grade each camp's standing and give out tickets and front-row places by it.
  7. First-timers are called virgins and are marked at the gate.
  8. Rich guests have paid up to 100,000 dollars for a ready-built camp, and the others hate them for it.
  9. A camp leader gives the pretty girl in the food line more than the man behind her.
  10. People dress up, show their bodies and build things to be looked at.
  11. More than 85 percent of returners come back for friends and belonging, and 63 percent in a study said the week changed them.
  12. Home again, many crash: tired, angry, low.
