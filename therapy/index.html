What it really is

A paid hour with a stranger, doing what your own people used to do.

Therapy is an hour a week with someone who listens, learns your whole story and is there again next week. For nearly all of human history the people you lived with did that, every day, and nobody sent a bill. Now most people live among strangers, so they rent it.It helps. What does the helping is mostly the person: how well you and the therapist get on predicts the result under every method, and for low mood, junior staff with no therapy training got the same results as accredited therapists. In the strictest trials the effect on low mood is small.Therapy gives you one person who knows you. It gives you nobody who needs you, and it leaves the life that made you feel this way as it was. Nothing in you is broken, so there is nothing in you to repair.

The part of you that calms down next to a person you trust is 165 million years old.Learn moreClose
Every mammal has it. Humans have existed for 300,000 years, and for nearly all of that time you lived among a few dozen people who knew your whole story, because they were in it. Then people started farming and settling down, and villages grew into towns and cities. That began 12,000 years ago. Now most people live among strangers. The person who knows you best is someone you pay by the hour, and the bill says treatment.

    Next to a person you trust, your body stands down.

    The science says

    Next to a person you trust, your body stands down. A threat looks smaller, and a fear is easier to train away.

    Holding the hand of someone you trust lowers the brain's reaction to a threat. In an experiment with 30 students, a fear that was trained away next to a photo of the person who supports them most did not come back the next day.

The cost-benefit analysis

Your brain does a cost-benefit analysis on being listened to. For nearly all of human history it came with living there. The people around you knew your whole story, because they were in it. Getting that today means building it: showing up for people for a long time, listening back, owing and being owed. An hour with a therapist costs money and asks nothing back. The brain takes the hour. It gives you one reliable person and nobody who needs you.

Next So now what

So now what

  1. If therapy helps you, keep it, and know what you are getting: one person who knows you. Use the hour to work out how to get more of them.
  2. Pick the person. If you and the therapist don't get on after a few sessions, change.
  3. For a fear that won't stop after something terrible, ask for therapy that goes back over what happened. That kind does a job of its own.
  4. Tell one person in your own life what you tell the therapist. Start with the part that is easiest to say.
  5. Join something where people need you to turn up: a team, a choir, a group of people with the same trouble.

If drugs came with the therapy: where no physical defect is the cause, the aim is to replace the drug with the real thing, which is people who know you, need you and give back. The drug comes down as those people come in, because the real thing is hard to build while you are on it. Never stop suddenly. Stopping some of these drugs, and alcohol, at once is dangerous, so do that with a doctor. If you are thinking about ending your life, tell someone today and call your local emergency number.

Why it wins. The need for a person who knows you explains 5 of the 12 details: the therapist who knows you better than anyone, the years people stay, the praise for the person, the click that decides everything and the loss when it ends. Helping each other explains the price, the therapist who asks nothing back and the subscription: the giving runs one way, so money stands in for the other half. Tracking where you stand explains why being treated as broken made people quit. It explains one detail, so it doesn't lead. The single hour, the disorder label and the many method names are explained by none of them. They are how the trade packages it.

The 12 details
  1. You pay a stranger: 70 to 100 dollars a week online, more in an office.
  2. It is one hour a week.
  3. The therapist comes to know you better than family, lovers or friends do.
  4. The therapist asks nothing back.
  5. People stay for years.
  6. What people praise is the person: she gets me, she is there.
  7. Whether you and the therapist click decides whether it works.
  8. Being treated as broken made people quit.
  9. It is sold as treatment for disorders.
  10. There are many named methods and no approval process.
  11. When it ends, everything that person knew about you is gone.
  12. Companies sell it by subscription, with messages at any hour.

  • For drinking, Alcoholics Anonymous, which is run by its own members, kept more people sober at one, two and three years than therapy given by clinicians.

What cuts against it

  • A volunteer is no match for a therapist. In 24 trials, an hour a week with an arranged volunteer lowered low mood a little, and it did worse than cognitive therapy in the few trials that compared the two.

    1 source: Mead et al. 2010. Full listClosetested many times Mead N, Lester H, Chew-Graham C, Gask L, Bower P (2010). Effects of befriending on depressive symptoms and distress: systematic review and meta-analysis. British Journal of Psychiatry 196.

  • For fear after danger, training counts. In 90 trials, every treatment that worked was a structured therapy given by professionals with special training, and therapy that goes back over the event beat supportive counselling.

What would prove this wrong

If people who live among dozens of others who know their whole story get as much out of therapy as people who have nobody, this page is wrong.

A study can settle this.

Strongest objection"Therapy saved my life." It may have. For a while one person knew you and stayed, and when you have nobody, that is a lot. Now ask why a stranger had to be paid to do it.
