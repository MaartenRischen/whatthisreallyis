The stage split photo

A promoter paid a lighting crew and a stylist to make one woman look like she wants you, and every repost sells that look again. The human sex drive stops for a fit, willing mate, and for most of our history she was one of a few dozen people standing right there.

Source: A photo of a performer on stage, shared with no caption, name or link

A swipe costs nothing and hands over a mate cue no real woman will ever return. Courting a real partner costs time, effort and the risk of hearing no, so the sex drive takes the free look every time. It was built for a camp of a few dozen people a man actually knew, where kin arranged most marriages and character and hard work counted. This woman is shown to a night arena and then to a feed, lit on a stage and shot close up from audience level.

The look goes nowhere. In a camp, people who knew you watched what you did and talked about it. Here nobody who knows the viewer sees him stop, the performer never learns he exists, and a second photo of the same outfit already waits at the edge of the screen. The sex drive is one of the oldest motives humans have, and it cannot learn that a photo is not a woman within reach.

The money goes to the people who built the show. Whoever sells the show and the feed that carries the repost both get paid for his attention.

How do we know?

What backs it

  1. Sex drive switches on from what a person sees, and attraction pulls mating effort toward the most attractive partner in view.

    • seen in animals E178 Fisher 2016, E424 Tinbergen 1951
    • argued, not yet tested E644 Agmo & Laan 2022

  2. Animals respond more strongly to cues blown up past nature than to the real thing.

    • seen in animals E424 Tinbergen 1951

  3. Men are built to overread women's sexual interest, because missing a chance to mate cost more than a wasted glance.

    • tested once E43 Haselton & Buss 2000
    • argued, not yet tested E459 Haselton & Buss 2000, E42 Haselton & Buss 2000

  4. For most of human history people lived day to day among a few dozen familiar faces.

    • tested many times EEA-SOC-03 Band / camp size (a few dozen adults; ~25-50), E754 Marlowe 2005, E404 Dunbar 2010
    • contested EEA-SOC-01 Social network layers (~5/15/50/150)

  5. In most forager societies kin arranged marriages, and character and hard work counted in a spouse.

    • tested many times E690 Apostolou 2007
    • tested once E692 Marlowe 2004
    • argued, not yet tested E691 Walker et al. 2011

  6. The sexual and attachment systems settle with a real partner, a bond that holds and children raised.

    • argued, not yet tested E173 Fisher 2016, E174 Fisher 2016, E193 Fisher 2016

  7. Everyday conduct was watched by people who knew you, and that record bore on allies and marriage prospects.

    • tested many times E373 Boehm 1999
    • argued, not yet tested EEA-SOC-09 Behavioral observability / reputation visibility, E375 Boehm 1999

  8. Socially important information came face to face from known people whose track record you could weigh.

    • tested many times EEA-INFO-02 Gossip and narrative flow as reputation and norm transmission
    • tested once E720 Wiessner 2014
    • argued, not yet tested EEA-INFO-01 Signal provenance and accountability (information from known, reputation-bearing, face-to-face sources)

  9. Lasting standing in forager bands came from prestige others chose to give.

    • tested many times E689 Garfield et al. 2019, E688 Boehm 1993
    • contested EEA-SOC-10 Leadership and dominance (no rulers; prestige by consent; exit available)

    • the source

What cuts against it

  1. The supernormal-stimulus findings are animal data, and applying them to human images is a modern extension. In modern samples, the looks people say they want barely predict whom they actually like after meeting.

    • seen in animals E424 Tinbergen 1951
    • contested E757 Eastwick et al. 2014

  2. Most marriages in the forager record were arranged by kin, so attention to the most attractive woman in view may not map onto how mates were found.

    • tested many times E690 Apostolou 2007
    • argued, not yet tested E691 Walker et al. 2011

  3. Ancestral body display to an audience has no forager record in Cor; the sexual-selection thesis behind display as a mating signal is argued, summary-level and limited to courtship traits.

    • argued, not yet tested E588 Miller 2000, E591 Miller 2000, E592 Miller 2000

  4. The image has no caption, so nothing states the viewer's reaction; moral condemnation runs on a separate and contested system.

    • tested many times E105 Landy & Goodwin 2015
    • argued, not yet tested E597 Tybur et al. 2013
