The miming bassist

Every human wants to be the best at something. For two million years that meant the best of maybe a hundred people, and a talented guy who worked hard could get there. Online there's one top spot for everyone alive, so he faked his way into it. When an English YouTuber caught him, it stopped being about bass. Argentina defended its war veteran, Sapko brought up Maradona, and both sides picked a team like it was 1982.

Source: Ultimate Guitar, 28 Oct 2026

Famous players praised and shared the clips, and Sapko says Billy Sheehan was among the few who saw through them. Nobody watching a clip can see the stage, and the prize on offer is the top spot among every player alive. For most of human history the people judging your skill watched you play every day, and the best of the camp was a prize years of practice could win.

Decades of practice buy the best of a small group, while a pedal and a backing track, if Sapko is right, buy the look of the best on earth for almost no work. The drive to be the best aims at whatever top spot it is shown, so that shortcut reads as a good deal. If he mimed, that is on him, and someone who says they played in his band is now accusing him.

Exposure runs on the same drive, because exposing a suspected cheat earns the catcher standing. In a camp the people who saw the deed kept the record in daily gossip, and a man who lost face could move camp and live it down. Here Sapko says a copyright strike took his first video down, and the source never says who is behind it. He posts a recap on October 27, other channels cover it, Sapko says critics get blocked, and nobody who watched both men can give a verdict.

He isn't disturbed, because faking to keep your rank is as human as it gets.

What they get wrong. Everyone's arguing about whether he lied. He did. Nobody asks why a man who can play would fake it.

How do we know?

What backs it

  1. In the few societies with detailed data, better hunters had more children than other men in their group.

    • tested many times E770 Smith 2004, E689 Garfield et al. 2019
    • tested once E771 Apicella 2014

  2. Across non-industrial societies, foragers included, higher-status men had more children.

    • tested many times E731 von Rueden & Jaeggi 2016
    • tested once E623 von Rueden et al. 2011

  3. Hunting skill kept rising into adulthood and peaked around the early thirties.

    • tested many times E744 Koster et al. 2020

  4. Forager camps held a few dozen people inside nested layers out to about 150.

    • tested many times EEA-SOC-03 Band / camp size (a few dozen adults; ~25-50), E754 Marlowe 2005, E404 Dunbar 2010
    • contested EEA-SOC-01 Social network layers (~5/15/50/150)

  5. Prestige is respect freely given to the skilled, and it lasted only while others chose to listen.

    • tested many times E689 Garfield et al. 2019
    • argued, not yet tested E122 Henrich 2016
    • contested EEA-SOC-10 Leadership and dominance (no rulers; prestige by consent; exit available)

  6. Camps kept a running record of who lied and shirked through gossip, ridicule and shunning.

    • tested many times E373 Boehm 1999
    • tested once E720 Wiessner 2014
    • argued, not yet tested E375 Boehm 1999, EEA-SOC-09 Behavioral observability / reputation visibility

  7. People could leave a camp for another and live a bad patch down.

    • tested once EEA-SOC-05 Membership turnover (camp dissolves and reconstitutes; most campmates change between survey waves)
    • argued, not yet tested E677 Woodburn 1982

  8. The miming claims, the copyright strike, the praise from famous players and the blocking all come from Sapko's side, with no reply from the accused.

    • the source

What cuts against it

  1. The hunting-skill link to reproductive success is directly challenged by a later study of foraging performance, and hunting reputations track real skill only noisily. Among forager-horticulturalists, dominance as well as prestige went with higher fertility.

    • tested many times E770 Smith 2004, E731 von Rueden & Jaeggi 2016
    • tested once E771 Apicella 2014, E623 von Rueden et al. 2011

  2. Big-game hunts rarely succeeded, so effort did not always pay off in camp, which limits how reliable being the best of the camp was as a prize.

    • tested many times E770 Smith 2004, E731 von Rueden & Jaeggi 2016
    • contested E810 Hawkes et al. 2014

  3. Late Pleistocene social life was probably more varied than the egalitarian mobile band; some forager groups had inequality and seasonal hierarchy.

    • contested E756 Singh & Glowacki 2022

  4. Cor holds no forager record of how bands treated a faked skill display. The cheater-detection evidence is lab-based and contested, and the gossip record rests on one population.

    • tested once E720 Wiessner 2014
    • contested E579 Cosmides 1989, E580 Cosmides 1989

  5. The source rests on unverified claims and Cor cannot tell whether he mimed. A false accusation would change what the cheater-detection signal reported on.

    • tested many times E460 Fehr & Gächter 2002
