What really is one in four teens never active? whatreally.is/one-in-four-teens-never-active

One in four teens never exercises, but nobody moves for a WHO target; people move when there's somewhere real to go.

Then For 300,000 years, nobody exercised.

The body spends energy on movement only when something real is out there to move for, and for a 2026 teen that thing now sits on the phone.

Teens who don't exercise

A teenager in 2026 can see every friend, have fun and eat dinner without taking a step. The body is built to stay alive and have children, and it never spends energy on a step that buys nothing.

Source: Euronews, 4 Nov 2026

Fifteen years of a WHO target moved the global average nowhere: 2.7 active days a week in 2010 and in 2025. Even the best countries, Finland, Canada and Serbia, reach only 4.5 to 4.8 days, far below a camp where moving fed you every day. No country or region is on track for the target, because what the body used to walk for now sits indoors.

For most of human history eating meant walking somewhere: adults covered roughly 9 to 15 km a day getting food, water and firewood, and even young children gathered a good share of their own food. Moving was how you got fed, and between trips people rested a lot. A camp child spent the day on foot in a self-run mixed-age group, with no timetable. A 2026 teen gets friends and food through a phone, sits through a school day adults timetabled, and rides to class on a route adults picked. The sum is simple: staying in costs nothing today, and the 60 minutes on offer buys a health promise decades away, so the body keeps the energy.

Girls fall furthest behind as they get older, averaging 2.4 active days against three for boys. In forager bands children's play gave way to food-getting work as they grew, mostly in mixed groups of boys and girls. Adolescence was when hard skills like hunting got taught. Now childhood play ends and nothing that moves replaces it, for girls and boys alike.

What they say it is

They say

“including restrictive gender norms, safety concerns and a lack of suitable activities”

The study authors, quoted by Euronews

Teens miss the WHO's 60 minutes because of weak policy funding, too few open spaces and safe walking or cycling routes, and for girls restrictive gender norms, safety concerns and a lack of suitable activities, so governments must act faster with tailored policies.

We say

The body spends energy on movement only when something real is out there to get, and a 2026 teen's friends, fun and food all come to the sofa.

What they get wrong. Funding, parks, safe streets and gender norms only decide where a teen could move, and faster government programmes still offer a 60-minute block that gets the body nothing today, so the study never asks why the body would bother.

So now what?

  1. 1

    Hand teens real errands that need their feet, like walking to buy the family's groceries and carrying them home.

  2. 2

    Get a mixed-age group of kids meeting outside at the same place and time every week, run by the kids themselves, so friends are out there to go to.

  3. 3

    Let teens walk or cycle to school with a friend on a route they pick themselves.

What backs it

  1. For most of human history moving always bought food, and the body spends energy on movement that gets it something and saves it otherwise.

    • tested many times EEA-DIET-03 Acquisition effort (calories are physically worked for), EEA-SUBS-01 Daily incidental movement load, E197 Lieberman 2021
    • tested once E196 Lieberman 2021, E289 Raichlen et al. 2020

  2. Forager adults covered roughly 9 to 15 km a day on foot getting food, water and firewood.

    • tested many times EEA-SUBS-01 Daily incidental movement load
    • tested once E196 Lieberman 2021

  3. Food had to be walked to, dug, hunted and processed before it was eaten.

    • tested many times EEA-DIET-03 Acquisition effort (calories are physically worked for)

  4. Even young forager children gathered a considerable amount of their own food, and play gave way to work as they grew.

    • tested once E787 Crittenden et al. 2013, E753 Boyette 2016
    • contested E716 Lew-Levy et al. 2017

  5. Forager children played and learned mostly in self-run, mixed-gender, mixed-age groups.

    • tested many times EEA-CARE-03 Mixed-age child playgroups as developmental input
    • tested once E785 Lew-Levy et al. 2020
    • contested E716 Lew-Levy et al. 2017

  6. Forager adults taught complex skills such as hunting mainly in adolescence.

    • contested E716 Lew-Levy et al. 2017

  7. Foragers rested about as many hours a day as industrial adults, much of it squatting or ground-sitting.

    • tested once E289 Raichlen et al. 2020
    • argued, not yet tested E288 Raichlen et al. 2020, E201 Lieberman 2021

  8. Exercise as a set-aside, prescribed health dose is a modern behaviour.

    • argued, not yet tested E198 Lieberman 2021, E199 Lieberman 2021

What cuts against it

  1. Forager activity was modest, only about 15 percent above a desk worker's overall, and foragers sat as many hours a day as industrial adults; how many hours foragers worked is contested.

    • tested many times E197 Lieberman 2021
    • tested once E289 Raichlen et al. 2020, E761 Hawkes & O'Connell 1981
    • contested EEA-SUBS-04 Daily subsistence work-hours (contested), E714 Sahlins 1972

  2. The Hadza activity figures cover adults in one population, and no Cor record gives forager adolescents' movement, so the teen-specific ancestral level is a gap.

    • tested once E715 Raichlen et al. 2017, E196 Lieberman 2021

  3. The forager play and work records cover children up to 16 in mixed groups. No Cor record gives adolescent forager girls' movement or a reason it would differ from boys', so the girls' gap on this page rests on the source's numbers alone.

    • tested once E785 Lew-Levy et al. 2020, E753 Boyette 2016

  4. The link between digital technology use and teen wellbeing is contested and small, and no Cor record shows phones displace teen activity; the phone step is the frame's derivation and has not been measured.

    • tested once E884 Kelly et al. 2018
    • contested E885 Orben & Przybylski 2019

  5. Play-learning in children is contested: one forager group's children did less rough-and-tumble than neighbouring farmers' children, and a review found weak evidence that pretend play causes the gains credited to it.

    • tested once E753 Boyette 2016
    • contested E716 Lew-Levy et al. 2017, E762 Lillard et al. 2013

  6. Boys' higher activity matches a modern-sample finding that rough-and-tumble play is more common in boys; that is not a forager finding.

    • tested many times E790 Pellegrini & Smith 1998

  7. Late Pleistocene social life was probably more varied than the nomadic-egalitarian model, so free movement and mixed-age play may not be the universal default.

    • tested many times E686 Kelly 2013
    • contested E756 Singh & Glowacki 2022
